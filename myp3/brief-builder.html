The Brief Builder

A one-page toolkit for turning your research — and your analysis of existing apps — into a design brief: one connected statement that names your app, says what it does, and cites every source it's built on.

MYP 3 Command term: Develop No new research beyond this point Cite every source you use
01

What Is a Design Brief?

A design brief is a short, connected statement that pulls together everything you found out across Strands i–iii — the problem and who it affects, what your research plan told you, and what you learned from analysing existing apps — and states, with sources cited, which app you're going to design and why.

Think of it as the bridge between your investigation (Strands i–iii, where you proved a real problem exists and studied how other apps handle similar problems) and your ideas (Criterion B, where you actually design the screens and features). The design brief is the last stop before you start designing — and every claim in it should point back to evidence you already have.

Golden rule

The task already tells you the format — you're designing an app. What you haven't said yet is which app, what it actually does, or who it's for. Strand iv is the first place in your whole project where you say that. Before this strand, your job was only to investigate the problem (Strand i), carry out your research plan (Strand ii), and study other apps (Strand iii) — never to describe your own solution.

02

Why Do We Write One?

It connects three strands. Your problem, your research plan findings, and your app analysis get pulled into one clear statement.

It keeps you on track. Every screen and feature in Criterion B should trace back to this exact brief.

It proves your sources. A brief with no citations reads like an opinion; a brief with citations reads like an investigation.

Real UX teams do this first. Before a design studio sketches a single screen, someone writes the brief the whole team builds from.

03

How Do We Write One?

Use the 3-Part Brief Builder. Every design brief you write, for the rest of your MYP Design journey, can follow this same shape.

1

Connect Your Research

Pull together the ONE most important thing each earlier strand told you: the problem from Strand i, what your research plan (Strand ii) confirmed, and what analysing an existing app (Strand iii) showed you should carry forward or avoid. Name a source every time — not just “refer appendix” on its own.

“My research showed that ___ (problem/audience), confirmed by ___ (Strand ii finding, cite source). Analysing ___ (existing app) showed I should carry forward ___ but avoid ___ (cite source).”
2

Design Question

Turn your connected insight into a challenge, starting with “How might we...?” This keeps the question open — it does not name your app yet.

“How might we ___ (do something) so that ___ (audience) can ___ (benefit)?”
3

Design Statement

Now, finally, name the app: what it's called (or its type), its core feature, and who it's for — based directly on your insight and question. This is the first time your app appears anywhere in the project.

“I am going to design an app called ___ that will ___ (core feature), so that ___ (audience) can ___ (benefit).”
04

Three Example Design Briefs

Read all three. Each one connects a problem, a research finding, and an app analysis — with sources named — before naming ONE app. You'll analyse them properly on the Task Sheet.

Example 1 — Where Did It Go? School lost property

The problem, in short: students who lose belongings around school have no way to check if someone's found them, other than asking around.

InsightMy research showed that 16 out of 22 students surveyed had lost an item at school this term, confirmed by a lost-property audit with the front office finding that only 1 in 5 lost items are ever claimed (Refer Appendix A2). Analysing the school's existing paper lost-property log showed items go unclaimed mainly because students don't know it exists — a gap I should avoid repeating.
QuestionHow might we help students check for lost items quickly so that they don't have to ask around or give up on finding them?
StatementI am going to design an app called FoundIt that lets students search and report lost items by photo and location, so that students can find their belongings without relying on word of mouth.
Example 2 — Skip the Line Canteen queues

The problem, in short: students queuing at the canteen during the short lunch break often don't get served before the bell.

InsightIn a class survey, 18 out of 25 students said they'd missed part of a lesson because of canteen queues (Refer Appendix B1). Interviewing canteen staff confirmed the busiest 10 minutes see triple the normal order rate. Analysing a pre-order app used by a nearby school showed pre-ordering cuts counter time significantly, but its confusing menu screen is something I should avoid.
QuestionHow might we reduce how long students spend queuing at the canteen so that they don't lose lesson time?
StatementI am going to design an app called QuickBite that lets students pre-order and pay for canteen items from their classroom, so that students can collect their food immediately instead of queuing.
Example 3 — Study Match Peer tutoring

The problem, in short: students struggling in a subject don't know which peers could help them, so requests for help happen randomly or not at all.

InsightA survey of 30 students found 21 wanted peer help in at least one subject but didn't know who to ask (Refer Appendix C3). A teacher interview confirmed informal peer tutoring already happens but is entirely word-of-mouth. Analysing a homework-help forum app showed anonymous posting increases participation, but its lack of subject filtering makes it hard to use — a feature to avoid.
QuestionHow might we connect students who need help with peers who can give it, so that requests don't rely on chance conversations?
StatementI am going to design an app called StudyMatch that lets students list subjects they can help with or need help in, and matches requests automatically, so that students can find peer support without waiting for a chance conversation.
