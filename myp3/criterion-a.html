The flow

Four quick notes, then one paragraph

1

Context

What's normal, common, or already true right now, before your specific problem comes in.

2

Target Audience

Who exactly is affected, and why this group specifically.

3

Problem (with evidence)

The problem, why it happens, and at least one fact, statistic, or survey result.

4

The Need

What happens if it's not solved, and why a solution matters.

These four parts are quick notes only. You write them up as one connected paragraph in the final answer — never as four separate answers.

In detail

What goes into each part

Part 1

Context

  • Background on your topic — what's normal, common, or already true right now, before your specific problem comes in
  • You can frame this using the unit's concepts (Development, Evaluation, Adaptation) if it helps

Part 2

Target Audience

  • Who exactly is affected (age/group), and why this group, specifically
  • Naming a precise audience strengthens your answer, even though MYP 3 doesn't require one to be formally “specified”

Part 3

Problem (with evidence)

  • The specific problem, and why it happens (the cause)
  • At least one fact, statistic, or survey result

Part 4

The Need

  • What happens if it's not solved (the impact)
  • Why a solution matters — backed by evidence

Writing it up

Turn your notes into one connected paragraph

Don't just answer the four parts separately. Include at least one piece of evidence and say where it came from.

To…Use a phrase like…
Start“[Group of people] often experience…”
Explain cause“This happens because…”
Add evidence“For example… / According to [source]…” — “This shows that…”
Finish“Therefore, a solution is needed because…”

Worked example

From the department's template

Context + Target Audience + Problem (with evidence) + The Need, written as one paragraph.

Read the worked exampleLost-and-found app · one paragraph

Many students at our school carry multiple bags and personal items between classes every day, often in busy corridors or crowded lockers. Grade 8 and 9 students in particular are affected, especially during the morning rush and the lunch period, when the library and cafeteria are at their busiest and belongings are most likely to be set down and forgotten. This has led to a growing lost-and-found problem: items like water bottles, pouches, and stationery are frequently left behind and rarely find their way back to their owner. This happens mainly because there is no quick way for a student to report a lost item or check if it's been found. In a short survey of 30 students in Grades 8–9, 22 said they had lost a personal item at school in the last month, and only 6 of those items were ever recovered. This matches a 2023 article on school lost-property systems, which found that most schools recover less than 40% of lost items when there's no digital way to log or search for them. As a result, students lose money replacing items and feel frustrated, sometimes losing things they can't easily replace. Therefore, a solution is needed that gives students a fast, simple way to report and search for lost items, reducing the time and money wasted on belongings that are often sitting just a few rooms away.

Why this works: Context opens it (corridors, lockers, morning rush). Target Audience is named precisely (Grade 8–9). The Problem comes with two pieces of evidence — a primary survey (22 of 30) and a secondary source (the 2023 article) — not just one. The Need closes the paragraph by tying straight back to the evidence just given, using “Therefore”.

The flow

Two parts

1

Map the plan

Mind map or flowchart: Primary and Secondary, each split into Sources and Purpose.

2

Build the table

Every question ranked, typed, sourced, and justified.

7–8 needs both — the mind map/flowchart and the table, not either one alone.

In detail

What goes into the plan

Part 1

Map the plan

  • Show your plan as a mind map or flowchart
  • Split it into Primary and Secondary, then split each of those into Sources and Purpose

Part 2

Build the table

  • Questions must be open — start with What, Which or How, never Is, Does or Can
  • Name the actual source and its appendix number
  • The last column says what you will do differently once you know the answer
Table columns Question → Priority → Type → Source → Justification

Priority — band + rank, e.g. High 1, Medium 2, Low 3. Rank within bands: High 1, High 2, High 3, then Medium 1, 2, 3, then Low 1, 2, 3. High priority questions are the ones you must answer before you can design anything.

Type — Primary or Secondary. Balance both across your plan.

Source — name the exact source (person, website, book) + appendix number.

Justification — include all three: (1) why this is a gap in your knowledge, (2) how it helps solve the design problem, (3) what you'll do differently once you know it.
Unresolved conflict — question count. The template says “Minimum five questions.” The real TSC's 7–8 descriptor requires a minimum of 7 prioritized, open research questions. The template undercounts what's needed for the top band. Until Dev confirms, treat 7 as the real minimum — it still fits inside the existing 5-area × 3-row structure, just spread across more of the rows.

Choose your method

Digital or physical?

This unit is an app, so CPFC is the main method — the other three are here for whenever a product is physical, or when you want to add a market-position read.

MethodUse it whenBest suited to
CPFCAnalysing an app, website or digital serviceDigital products — something with screens and a task to complete
ACCESS FMAnalysing a manufactured physical productPhysical products — furniture, packaging, devices, tools
PPCUnderstanding how a physical object is builtPhysical products where construction and mechanism matter most
SWOTJudging a product or brand's position in the marketEither — useful alongside one of the three above, not instead of it

You do not have to pick only one. Use CPFC, ACCESS FM or PPC as your main method for every product, then add a short SWOT if you want to judge its market position too.

Method 1

CPFC — for a digital product

Choose one task a real user would do on it, and analyse that journey — not the whole product.

C — Components Screenshot every screen on the task, in order, then label what is on each one.

P — Purposes This component exists so that the user can ___.

F — Flow From [screen] the user taps ___ to reach [screen]. It takes ___ steps in total.

C — Complexities This breaks down when ___, because ___.

Do not skip the hidden states. Deliberately break it — submit nothing, lose the connection, search for something that does not exist. Screenshot the empty state, the loading state and the error state. These are components too.

ScreenComponent and purposeFlow — what happens nextComplexity noticed
Menu screenSearch field — lets the user find a dish without scrollingTap a dish card to open Dish detailNo results shown if the search finds nothing

One example row from the template — a food-delivery app, not this unit's app. Build your own row per screen.

Method 2

ACCESS FM — for a manufactured physical product

One row per aspect: [Aspect] — strength: ___ — weakness: ___.

The eight aspects Aesthetics · Cost · Customer · Environment · Size · Safety · Function · Materials

Every aspect needs both a strength and a weakness. If you cannot find a weakness, look harder — no product scores perfectly on every aspect.

Method 3

PPC — how a physical object is built

Place an image of the product in the centre of the page, then work outward in three passes.

P — Parts Label each part with its correct name, or a name that describes it.

P — Purpose This part exists so that ___.

C — Complexities [Part A] and [Part B] work together to ___. This creates a weakness because ___.

Method 4

SWOT — market position

Use alongside one of the three methods above. One sentence per box: this product's [strength/weakness/opportunity/threat] is ___, because ___.

SWOT alone is not enough for this strand. It tells you the product's position, not how it works. Pair it with CPFC, ACCESS FM or PPC — never submit SWOT on its own.

Every product

Conclusion, per product

Formula: from this product I will carry forward ___, and I will avoid ___.

All pictures, text or anyone else's work must be cited — very important.

What goes in the brief

Connect, don't re-cap

  • Connect the research from strand i — the problem and your target audience
  • Connect the research from strand ii — what your primary and secondary research found
  • Connect the research from strand iii — what the existing products taught you
  • Cite every piece of research you refer to
  • State what you are going to design and make
  • Say who it is for, and why
Unresolved conflict — design question & statement. The real TSC's 7–8 descriptor explicitly requires the brief to “present a design question and a clear design statement.” The template's Strand iv box currently has no prompt for this at all — the template itself is missing something students need to reach the top band. Write both in anyway: a design question (“How might we…?”) and a design statement (what will be made and for whom). Recommend adding a prompt for this into the Strand iv template when it's next revised, rather than treating it as optional.

Worked example

From the department's template

Reproduced from a real portfolio. It borrows a different product context (a snack, not an app) to show the connective structure — secondary research, primary research, market research, a summary of insights, and the product brief, each one traceable back to the finding above it.

Read the worked exampleSecondary + primary + market research → brief

Secondary Research Brief

Every serving of Snacktive must present an optimal balance of macronutrients and indispensable micronutrients to answer the nutritional demand of children 10–14 years old. Preferably, it must carry 10–15g protein, 6–8g of fiber, and 4–6 mg iron to enhance muscle development, promote digestive regularity, and assure proper delivery of oxygen by the body. To preserve nutrition with minimum fat consumption, the most appropriate cooking techniques would be baking, air-drying, or roasting since deep-frying incorporates excess fats and boiling can destroy water-soluble vitamins.

Primary Research Brief

For Indian children between the ages of 10–14, the best macronutrient composition should be about 50–55% carbohydrates, 20–25% protein, and 20–25% good fats for growth and energy requirements. For better iron absorption of black beans and spinach, complementing them with vitamin C-containing foods such as citrus fruits or bell peppers works well.

Market Research (existing products)

The Indian health food market is growing day by day, but nevertheless, there remains a large sector that has been left untapped — the savoury health snack market. That leaves only 1 player in the savoury healthy snack space, Snackible, which is able to attract a great deal of attention through innovative flavours and combinations but still fails in exciting the average adolescent consumer.

Important Insights and Patterns Identified

  • A balanced ratio of carbohydrates, proteins, and fats to ensure sustained energy and support growth in the target age group
  • Vitamin C in aiding iron absorption from ingredients like black beans and spinach
  • Baking or air-frying while cooking the product to reduce fat quantity and preserve nutrients
  • Reducing anti-nutrients like phytates and oxalates by soaking, fermenting or sprouting millets and spinach
  • Using natural flavourings in the seasoning blends to minimise the introduction of unwanted chemicals
  • Legumes, whole grains, seeds and black beans in maximising the amount of protein and fibre in the snack

Product Brief

Snacking is the favourite pastime of the human population, something every single person engages in, and an opportunity to provide much-needed nutrition that hasn't been exploited as yet. The ideal delivery mechanism of these benchmarks would be a simple snack — something that can be eaten at any time of the day, at work or at home, during commutes as well.

It is essential that the product features a bright colour scheme, differentiating itself from the other snacks found in regular supermarkets in order to captivate the attention of the target audience. To further hone in the solution's ability to cater to the target group, it must be available in four different flavours, each attracting a different sector of the group.

Why this works: every claim in the Product Brief traces back to a finding stated above it — the macronutrient ratio, the iron-absorption pairing, the cooking method, all appear first in the research, then get carried into what will be made. Nothing in the brief is new information.
