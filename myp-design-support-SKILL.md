# MYP Design — subject knowledge and working method

This skill carries what a design teacher and I worked out together over many
sessions: how MYP Design is actually assessed, which frameworks serve which
strand, how to read a rubric correctly, and how to evaluate student writing
against it without inventing anything. Use it whenever the task touches MYP
Design, regardless of which school, which year group, or what file format is
requested.

---

## What MYP Design actually is

Four criteria, each with four strands, each strand marked 0 to 8 in bands of
two. The whole subject runs on the **design cycle**: identify a problem
(A), specify and design a solution (B), make it (C), test it (D).

| Criterion | Focus | The four strands, in shape |
|---|---|---|
| A — Inquiring and analysing | Justify that a problem is real and worth solving | Explain & justify need → research plan → analyse existing products → design brief |
| B — Developing ideas | Specify success, then design toward it | Specifications → range of ideas → chosen design, justified → planning drawings |
| C — Creating the solution | Make it, and account honestly for what changed | Plan → technical skill → the solution itself → justify changes |
| D — Evaluating | Test it, judge it, say what it meant | Testing methods → evaluate against spec → improvements → impact on client |

**Officially published rubrics exist for Years 1, 3 and 5 only.** MYP 4
assesses against the Year 5 descriptors. If a year group isn't 1, 3 or 5, ask
which published rubric the school uses rather than guessing — descriptors
tighten considerably between years (e.g. Year 1 strand i asks only to
*outline* a need; Year 5 asks to *explain and justify* it with cited
evidence).

## The one rule that overrides everything else here

**Schools customise the TSC (task-specific clarification).** What's in this
skill is the *general shape* of MYP Design — the published command terms and
band structure. A real school's version often adds specifics: minimum source
counts, named tools, product-count thresholds, department checklists with
their own wording.

**Never invent or guess a school's customised descriptor.** If asked to write
or evaluate against a TSC and the actual document hasn't been provided, ask
for it. A plausible-sounding descriptor that isn't the real one will mislead
a student who trusts it. This matters more than any other instruction in this
skill.

If two of a school's own documents conflict (a TSC says one number, a
checklist says another), say so plainly and flag it — don't quietly pick one.

## The command term ladder

Command terms are IB-wide, not school-specific, so these are safe to use
directly. The verb tells you the band before anything else does:

| Term | Definition | Typically appears at |
|---|---|---|
| List | A sequence of brief answers, no explanation | 1–2 |
| State | A specific name, value or brief answer, no explanation | 1–2 |
| Outline | A brief account or summary | 3–4 |
| Identify | Provide an answer from a number of possibilities | Across bands |
| Describe | A detailed account of a situation, event, pattern or process | Varies |
| Explain | A detailed account including reasons or causes | 5–6 |
| Construct | Display information in a diagrammatic or logical form | Varies (ii) |
| Analyse | Break down to bring out essential elements or structure | Varies (iii) |
| Develop | Improve incrementally, expand in detail, evolve to a more advanced state | Varies |
| Justify | Give valid reasons or evidence to support an answer or conclusion | 7–8 |
| Summarize | Abstract a general theme or major points | Varies (A iv) |
| Evaluate | Make an appraisal by weighing up strengths and limitations | Criterion D |
| Demonstrate | Make clear by reasoning or evidence, with examples | Criterion C |

**The gap between adjacent bands is usually the command term, not the
content.** A student explaining well but citing nothing is capped below
"justify" — the fix is evidence, not more writing. Always name which verb a
piece of work is actually performing before saying which band it's in.

## How to evaluate a student submission

This is the most-used task this skill supports. Follow this order:

1. **Get the actual TSC and checklist for that strand.** If not supplied, ask.
2. **Identify the command term the writing performs**, sentence by sentence
   if needed. A student can *explain* in one paragraph and merely *state* in
   the next — band per strand is bounded by the weakest link, not the best.
3. **Check every checklist item literally**, not by impression. "Cited
   copied text or pictures?" is either true or false — check it, don't infer
   it from overall quality.
4. **Separate genuine strengths from surface polish.** Long, well-written
   prose with no research behind it is not the same as short, evidenced
   writing. Flag data-only claims (no "so what") as a specific, fixable gap.
5. **State a band range, not a single number**, unless asked to commit to
   one — moderation is imprecise and a range is more honest.
6. **Name the single highest-leverage fix.** Not a list of ten
   improvements — the one change that would move the most marks fastest.
   Usually it's naming an audience, adding one piece of primary evidence, or
   adding a required visual/graphic organiser.
7. **Never fabricate a rubric quote.** Quote the real TSC wording given, or
   say clearly that generic descriptors are being used pending the real one.

## Frameworks by criterion and strand

Tools worth reaching for, organised by where they're used. Not every school's
TSC names all of these — check which are required versus optional.

### Criterion A — Inquiring and analysing

**Strand i (justify the need)**
- Problem hypothesis frame — turns an assumed problem into a falsifiable claim
- W³D organiser — who / why / where / data, one page
- Empathy map — says / thinks / does / feels, built from real interaction only
- Circle of Viewpoints — multiple stakeholder perspectives as a diagram
- Fishbone (Ishikawa) — six cause categories (People, Process, Place, Tools,
  Time, Rules), wide search before depth
- Five Whys — narrow and deep, symptom to root cause
- Problem tree — roots (causes) / trunk (core problem) / branches (effects);
  checks whether a student is designing for a symptom
- Scale ladder — local to global evidence search, in order
- Rule of three — a number, a voice, an observation; triangulates any claim
- Source card + OPVL (Origin, Purpose, Value, Limitation) — judging evidence
  quality, not just collecting it

**Strand ii (research plan)**
- Research areas before research questions (topic, then question)
- ACCESS FM as an area generator (Aesthetics, Cost, Customer, Environment,
  Size, Safety, Function, Materials)
- MoSCoW or forced ranking for prioritisation — never leave "everything is
  high priority" unchallenged

**Strand iii (analyse existing products)**
- ACCESS FM — physical products, strength/weakness per aspect
- PPC (Parts, Purpose, Complexities) — how a physical object is actually
  built, drawn onto an image in three passes
- CPFC (Components, Purposes, Flow, Complexities) — the same idea adapted
  for digital products; Flow is the addition, since an app only exists
  while someone moves through it. Deliberately test hidden states: empty,
  loading, error.
- SWOT — market position; pairs with one of the above, never stands alone
  for this strand
- Every product needs at least one genuine weakness. A product with none
  hasn't been analysed, only described.

**Strand iv (design brief)**
- Summarise, don't repeat — no new research belongs here
- A useful three-stage internal structure: research insights → design
  question ("How might we [X] so that [Y]?") → design statement (the broad
  blueprint, explicitly *not* the specification — that's Criterion B)
- Never name a product before this strand; strand i forbids it, strand iv
  requires it

### Criterion B — Developing ideas

**Strand i (specifications)**
- ACCESS FM, or a school's extended list (Target Market, Manufacturing,
  Time Scale are common additions)
- Every criterion should be a "shall" statement with a number or observable
  outcome, justified from named research, with a stated test method
- A success-criteria rubric (student writes their own 1–2 / 3–4 / 5–6 / 7–8
  descriptors per aspect) is a strong feature some schools expect implicitly
  even when the TSC doesn't spell it out — check real samples before
  assuming it's required
- OPVL-style reflection (Opinion, Proof, Limitation, Value) fits naturally
  here for justifying design direction

**Strand ii (design ideas)**
- Crazy 8s — eight sketches in eight minutes, breaks fixation on one idea
- SCAMPER — substitute, combine, adapt, modify, put to other use,
  eliminate, reverse
- Check the school's actual number required — "range" can mean anywhere
  from 3 to 8+ depending on the TSC; conflicting internal documents on this
  number are common, flag if found

**Strand iii (justify selection)**
- Weighted decision matrix against the specification — ideas as rows,
  "must" criteria as weighted columns
- The justification must reference the specification by number, not just
  general preference

**Strand iv (planning drawings)**
- Physical: dimensioned orthographic views + exploded assembly diagram
- Digital: navigation map, wireframes, or storyboard
- Peer test: could someone else actually build from this drawing alone?

### Criterion C — Creating the solution

- Production plan as a table: step, time, tools, materials, safety, quality
  check — not prose
- Gantt chart for showing genuinely efficient (not just sequential) time use
- Process log with dated photographs — evidence of skill happens in the
  documentation, not just the final object
- Modification log with a timestamp on every change — the "why" fades from
  memory fast; capture it the day it happens
- Plan-Do-Check-Act as a tight loop for testing during making, not only at
  the end

### Criterion D — Evaluating

- Testing methods matrix: one row per specification point, method, data
  type, and the success threshold — written *before* testing, not after
- Usability/think-aloud testing for anything with a user interacting with it
- Point-by-point specification audit: met / partly met / not met, each with
  evidence, each "not met" written up honestly rather than defended
- OPVL turned on the student's own data — judging their own sample size and
  method, not just external sources
- Before-and-after against the Criterion A baseline — the number collected
  in strand i becomes the benchmark impact is measured against
- What / So what / Now what for turning findings into a real, actionable
  improvement rather than a vague "make it better"

### Cross-cutting (used across multiple criteria)

- **PEEL** (Point, Evidence, Explain, Link) — any paragraph making a claim.
  The Link should point at a specification number or the target audience,
  not just trail off.
- **Rule of three** — a number, a voice, an observation, for any claim that
  needs to survive scrutiny.

## Common traps, by strand

Worth naming explicitly when giving feedback — these repeat across cohorts:

- **Solution smuggling in strand i** — "students need an app for X" is a
  solution wearing a problem's clothes. Rewrite as what a solution must *do*.
- **The invisible audience** — "everyone" or "people" is not a target
  audience. Push for one nameable group, one setting.
- **Data with no insight** — a statistic followed by nothing is collection,
  not analysis. Every number needs a "what this means for my users" after it.
- **"Refer Appendix C" as a citation** — not a citation. Source name, year,
  appendix number, every time.
- **Product analysis with no weaknesses** — nothing scores perfectly;
  absence of a weakness means the analysis stopped too early.
- **Specification with no test method** — makes Criterion D impossible
  later; the two criteria are structurally linked even though marked
  separately.
- **Changes justified after the fact, vaguely** — "I changed the material
  because it looked better" is not a justification. Push for the specific
  failure or finding that triggered the change.

## Style for anything written under this skill

- **Formulas over paragraphs for younger year groups or first drafts.**
  A fill-in-the-blank sentence structure ("The product shall ___ because
  ___") gets usable output faster than a paragraph of guidance that a
  student has to translate into their own writing.
- **Real extracts over invented examples, always, when source material
  exists.** A genuine sentence from a real (anonymised) student submission
  teaches more than a polished example written to be exemplary — including
  its flaws, clearly labelled.
- **Say what's missing as plainly as what's strong.** A worked example with
  only praise teaches nothing about the ceiling. Pair every "why this works"
  with a "what is missing" where one exists.
- **Red or bold text for the two or three things that actually cost marks**,
  not for everything. If everything is flagged, nothing is.
- **Never present a borrowed framework as the school's own invention.**
  If a technique traces to a named source (Project Zero's thinking
  routines, a specific published framework), say so. If adapting a
  copyrighted resource, check the licence before reproducing anything —
  CC BY-NC-ND permits unaltered redistribution with credit but not
  derivative redrawing; plain copyright permits neither without permission.

## What to ask for, if missing, before starting real work

- The exact TSC wording for the strand in question
- The department's own checklist, if one exists separately from the TSC
- Real (anonymised) student samples, if the task involves building teaching
  resources or exemplars
- Confirmation of year group, since Years 1, 3 and 5 rubrics genuinely differ
- Whether any reference material being adapted carries a licence restricting
  reuse
