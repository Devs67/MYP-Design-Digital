Writing Evaluative Statements

Criterion D, Strand ii: Analyse the data collected through authentic testing and critically evaluate the success of the solution against the design specification using mathematical tools.

Analyse: break down to bring out the essential elements, identify parts and relationships, and interpret information to reach conclusions. Evaluate: make an appraisal by weighing up strengths and limitations.

First study the two worked examples. Then complete the five case studies. In each one you must show your calculation, compare it with the specification, and write your own evaluative statement.

Tip: you can scroll back to the worked examples at any time.

Worked Examples: How to Write an Evaluative Statement

Study the following two examples before attempting the case studies. Notice how the mathematical calculations are used as evidence to evaluate the success of a solution against its design specification.

Example 1: The Water Bottle – Calculating the Mean

Design contextA student has designed a reusable water bottle for school students. The bottle is expected to keep drinking water cool throughout the school day. Design specificationThe bottle should maintain an average water temperature of 15°C or below after being used for three hours. Testing methodThe student filled the bottle with water and measured its temperature using a digital thermometer at the end of three hours. The test was repeated five times under identical conditions.
Testing data
TrialWater temperature (°C)
114
216
315
413
517

Step 1: Analyse the data

To calculate the mean: Mean = Sum of all readings ÷ Number of readings

= (14 + 16 + 15 + 13 + 17) ÷ 5
Mean = 15°C

Step 2: Compare with the design specification

Required temperature: 15°C or below. Actual mean temperature: 15°C.
The solution meets the design specification exactly.

Step 3: Write the evaluative statement

The water bottle successfully meets the design specification, as the average water temperature after three hours was 15°C, which is within the required limit of 15°C or below. This indicates that the bottle is effective in maintaining cool water for students throughout this period. However, the mean is exactly at the maximum permitted temperature, suggesting that there is little performance margin. Further improvements in insulation could help maintain an even lower temperature, particularly in warmer conditions.

Example 2: The Automatic Hand Sanitiser Dispenser – Percentage of Success

Design contextA student has designed an automatic hand sanitiser dispenser for school corridors. The dispenser uses an infrared sensor to detect a user's hand and release sanitiser automatically. Design specificationThe dispenser should detect a user's hand and release sanitiser successfully in at least 90% of testing trials. Testing methodThe student placed their hand under the sensor and observed whether the dispenser released sanitiser correctly. The test was repeated 20 times.
Testing data
Test outcomeNumber of trials
Sanitiser dispensed correctly17
Dispenser failed to respond3
Total trials20

Step 1: Analyse the data

To calculate the percentage of successful trials: Success rate (%) = (Successful trials ÷ Total trials) × 100

= (17 ÷ 20) × 100
Success rate = 85%

Step 2: Compare with the design specification

Required success rate: at least 90%. Actual success rate: 85%.
The solution does not meet the design specification.

Step 3: Write the evaluative statement

The automatic hand sanitiser dispenser does not fully meet the design specification, as it achieved a success rate of 85%, which is 5 percentage points below the required 90%. Although the dispenser functioned correctly in 17 out of 20 trials, the three unsuccessful attempts indicate that the infrared sensor may not consistently detect the user's hand. This could cause inconvenience and reduce the reliability of the dispenser in busy school corridors. The sensor sensitivity or positioning needs to be improved to achieve the required level of performance.

What should your evaluative statement include?

  1. Mathematical evidence: include the calculated result, such as the mean, percentage or error rate.
  2. Comparison: state whether the result meets the design specification.
  3. Evaluation: explain the strengths and limitations of the solution.
  4. User impact: explain how the results affect the intended user or the overall performance of the solution.

Important: do not simply write that a product is successful or unsuccessful. Support your judgement with numerical evidence and explain what the results mean.
