MYP 4 Design · Task-specific clarification

Criterion B — Developing ideas

What must be in each strand, the command term at every band, and the steps that turn research into a testable specification.

Strand i — objective Develop design specifications which clearly state the success criteria for the design of a solution.

Command terms — Criterion B, strand i

1–4ListGive a sequence of brief answers with no explanation.
5–8DevelopTo improve incrementally, elaborate or expand in detail. Evolve to a more advanced or effective state.
5–6OutlineGive a brief account or summary.
7–8ExplainGive a detailed account including reasons or causes.

Two jumps to notice. From 3–4 to 5–6 is list → develop. From 5–6 to 7–8 is outline → explain — which means saying why each criterion exists, traced back to your research, and naming how you will test it.

The flow

Four steps, in this order

One to two pages. Subheading: Design Specifications.

Step 1

Set your headings

ACCESS FM, adapted to your product.

Step 2

Write and justify each criterion

What it must do, and the research it came from.

Step 3

Name the testing method

How you will prove it was met.

Step 4

Build the success criteria

Your own 1–2 to 7–8 descriptors for each aspect.

Steps 3 and 4 are what your task-specific clarification adds at 7–8. They are also what makes Criterion D possible — the testing methods and thresholds you write here become the evaluation later.

In detail

What goes into each step

Every example below is a real extract from one of the two sample portfolios.

Step 1

Set your headings

  • Use ACCESS FM: Aesthetics, Cost, Customer, Environment, Size, Safety, Function, Materials
  • Adapt the headings to your product — drop what does not apply, add what does
  • Say in one line which format you are using
  • Every heading gets a paragraph, not a line
The eight headings Aesthetics · Cost · Customer · Environment · Size · Safety · Function · Materials

Your department checklist also asks about Target Market, Manufacturing and Time Scale. Add them as extra rows if your project needs them.

From a strong response

“Written below is the Success Criteria following the ACCESS FM format along with a description and its justification.”

What it does: one sentence naming the format before the table starts. A moderator immediately knows what they are reading.

From a strong response — adapted headings

Sample 2’s aspects: Aesthetics · Nutrition · Cost · Customer · Size · Safety · Materials · Flavour

What it does: this is ACCESS FM adapted for a food product. Environment was dropped; Nutrition and Flavour were added because Function means something different for a snack. Adapting the headings sensibly is stronger than forcing your product into eight words that do not fit.

Step 2

Write and justify each criterion

  • Say what the product shall do, with a number or a measurable outcome
  • Beside it, say why — trace it to a finding from Criterion A
  • Name the research: the survey, the interview, the product you analysed
  • A criterion with no research behind it is a preference, not a specification

From a strong response — sample 1, Function

Description — “The function of this board is to connect people over several board games. The product should be able to include several games in an innovative and convenient manner… The product should include sufficient storage space for the included game pieces.”

Justification — “Based on the problem that needs to be solved, the function has been determined… This will further mean that the games have to be multiplayer games and there have to be more than 5 games in one product along with sufficient storage.”

What it does: the justification does real work — it converts the problem statement into two countable requirements: multiplayer, and more than five games.

From a strong response — sample 2, Macronutrients

Specification — “Calories (350–400 kcal) · Protein (15g–20g) · Fat (10g–12g) · Carbohydrates (40g–45g) · Fibre (7g–10g)”

Justification — “15g of protein for every 100g of Snacktive consumed will appeal to the large numbers of athletes aged 10–14 years old. High protein quantities will also help bolster growth in the crucial ages of puberty. The additional 10g of fibre helps support digestive processes and improve gut health. This also caters to the significant iron deficiency observed in most Indian cities.”

What it does: every number is defended, and each defence names the user. Ranges rather than single values, which is realistic for a product still being designed.

What to avoid — sample 1, Environment

“The environment is extremely important for all of us and hence it is essential that we try and save the environment in every step of the way. And hence, this product should be eco-friendly.”

Why it is weaker: true, but it is opinion. Nothing in it comes from research and nothing in it can be measured. Compare with the same student’s Cost row, which cites a real $7–$25 price range.

Step 3

Name the testing method

  • A third column: how will you check this criterion was met?
  • The test must generate data — a number, a count, a rating, an expert view
  • Use more than one kind of test across the specification
  • These become your Criterion D strand i testing methods
Testing methods from the samples Customer surveys · Targeted questionnaires · Expert analysis · Laboratory testing · Weighing scale · Ruler · Interview · Self-evaluation · Client review

From a strong response — sample 2, three rows

Branding → “Customer Surveys: targeted questionnaires sent to the target audience would help understand the preferences of this demographic. Expert Analysis: analysing the design, the homogeneousness of the design components and the produced aesthetic using the help of a graphic designer will help understand the extent of changes required.”

Macronutrients → “Nutritional Analysis: through laboratory testing, the various macronutrient and micronutrient parameters can be determined.”

Size → “Weighing Scale: using a weighing scale would allow the mass of each portion to be determined. Ruler: a ruler would allow for the determination of the dimensions of each piece and package.”

What it does: three rows, three completely different kinds of data — opinion, laboratory measurement, and physical measurement. That is what “various testing methods” means in your task-specific clarification.

What sample 1 is missing

Sample 1 has only two columns: description and justification. There is no testing method anywhere in the specification.

Why it matters: without it, Criterion D has nothing to evaluate against. Sample 1 partly recovers this through its success criteria rubric, but naming the method explicitly is what the 7–8 descriptor asks for.

Step 4

Build the success criteria

  • For each aspect, write your own descriptors at 1–2, 3–4, 5–6 and 7–8
  • Say exactly what the product looks like at each level
  • Use numbers wherever you can — the same measure, at four thresholds
  • Write this before you design, so success is defined rather than decided afterwards
This is the piece students never think of on their own, and both samples do it. It turns “the product should be affordable” into a scale you can actually mark against.

From a strong response — sample 1, Aesthetics

7–8: “The board includes 5 or more board games. This board is only a shade of beige/brown to show the shade of the natural wood in the borders. Additionally, the board includes several various colours in most of the games.”

1–2: “The board includes less than 3 board games. The board does not follow the colour scheme of beige. The product includes no additional colours.”

What it does: the same three measures at both ends, with only the threshold changing. A reader can place any finished board on that scale without argument.

From a strong response — sample 2, Nutrition

7–8: “Protein content is between 18g and 20g. Fibre content is between 8g and 10g. Fat is between 10g and 12g, while carbohydrate content is between 43g and 45g. The calorie count of one serving is between 330 and 350 kcal.”

1–2: “Protein content is between 12g and 14g. Fibre content is between 2g and 4g. Fat is between 16g and 18g, while carbohydrate content is between 52g and 56g. The calorie count is between 400 and 420 kcal.”

What it does: fully quantified at every band. Nothing is left to judgement — a laboratory result places the product on the scale automatically.

Snacktive · healthy savoury snack, ages 10–14Adapted ACCESS FM · three columns including testing method · quantified rubric

Aesthetics

SpecificationJustificationTesting method
Branding — clean, modern and minimalistic design with warm tones of brown, green, and accents of blue and purple. Able to stand out on retail shelves with boldly placed text like ‘High-Protein’ or ‘High-Fibre’. Packaging — sustainable, eco-friendly packaging in tones of an earthy brown. Resealable pouch. Compact design. Inclusion of labels such as FSSAI approved or Organic. Includes a nutrition label marking each nutrition quantity. A clean, modern and minimalistic aesthetic provides a premium and modern brand feel, which helps in creating appeal for the product. Labels like ‘Organic’ and ‘High Protein’ can help attract health conscious customers without the need for expensive marketing campaigns. Sustainable and eco-friendly packaging options will allow consumers to feel good about their purchase, while the reusable nature of the pouch would allow the product to be consumed over a period of time determined by the customer. Customer Surveys — targeted questionnaires sent to the target audience would help understand the preferences of this demographic. Expert Analysis — analysing the design, the homogeneousness of the design components and the produced aesthetic using the help of a graphic designer will help understand the extent of changes required.

Nutrition

SpecificationJustificationTesting method
Macronutrients — Calories 350–400 kcal, Protein 15–20g, Fat 10–12g, Carbohydrates 40–45g, Fibre 7–10g. Micronutrients — Iron 3–5mg (15–25% DV), Sodium 200–250mg, Potassium 300–400mg, Calcium 50–100mg, Magnesium 5–75mg. Vitamins — A 200–250 IU, E 3–5mg. All values per 100g serving. 15g of protein per 100g will appeal to athletes aged 10–14. High protein quantities help bolster growth in the crucial ages of puberty. The additional 10g of fibre supports digestive processes and improves gut health, and caters to the significant iron deficiency observed in most Indian cities. Almost 25% of the daily recommended iron intake helps combat deficiencies faced by most Indian citizens, particularly female consumers. Nutritional Analysis — through laboratory testing, the various macronutrient and micronutrient parameters can be determined.

Cost

SpecificationJustificationTesting method
Production — raw ingredients Rs 10–12, labour Rs 3–4, utilities Rs 1–2, quality control Rs 0.5–1, overheads Rs 2–3. Total Rs 16–20. Packaging — primary Rs 4–5, secondary Rs 2–4. Total Rs 6–9. Marketing and distribution — advertising Rs 2–4, logistics Rs 3–4. Total Rs 5–8. MRP — total cost + 30%, Rs 45–60. To ensure the product serves the majority of the Indian population, prices must be pinned to the lower end of the spectrum. A price of Rs 60 will ensure the product is viewed as ‘premium’ while still allowing the masses to access it weekly. Snacktive falls into the same price category as Yoga Bar, The Whole Truth and Nourishables, pricing between Rs 50–80 per 100g, while being significantly cheaper than imported health foods at Rs 100–150. Cost Analysis — analysis of the cost of raw ingredients bought in bulk alongside the changing costs of production.

SpecificationJustificationTesting method
Primary — adolescents aged 10–14. Secondary — all age groups, all genders, middle to upper class with disposable income. Psychographics — athletes and sports enthusiasts, parents seeking nutritious alternatives, health conscious citizens, working professionals. Geographics — Tier 1 and Tier 2 cities (Hyderabad, Mumbai, Delhi, Bengaluru, Chennai), online platforms. The target audience of primarily children aged 10–14 is crucial to the success of future generations due to the widespread influence of unhealthy snacks. Snacktive solves the iron and protein deficiency crisis found all over India by integrating these nutrients into the very core of the snack. Interview — with members of the target audience to understand varying perspectives. Survey — a larger demographic means a better understanding of the demographic overall.

Thinking tools

Frameworks for each step

Tools marked named in TSC appear in the task-specific clarification itself. The others are optional, but each one produces something a step needs.

Step 1

Set your headings

2 tools

ACCESS FM

Named in TSC

Produces: full coverage rather than only the headings you find easy.

  1. Aesthetics, Cost, Customer, Environment, Size, Safety, Function, Materials.
  2. Adapt to your product — drop what does not apply, add what does.
  3. State which format you are using in one line before the table.

Specification mind map

Produces: the raw material for each paragraph.

  1. One branch per heading.
  2. Under each, note every Criterion A finding that touches it.
  3. Empty branches show where your research was thin.
Step 2

Write and justify

2 tools

Shall statements

Produces: criteria you can test.

  1. Write “The product shall…” every time.
  2. Attach a number, a range or an observable outcome.
  3. Ranges are fine and often more honest than a single value.

Research traceability

Produces: the justification column, without inventing anything.

  1. Beside each criterion, name the research question or source it came from.
  2. A blank cell means it came from your taste, not your research.
  3. Either find the evidence or remove the criterion.
Step 3

Name the testing method

2 tools

Method chooser

Produces: the right test for each criterion rather than a survey for everything.

  1. How much or how many → measurement, weighing, counting.
  2. Do people like it → survey, interview, client review.
  3. Is it technically correct → laboratory test, expert analysis.

Data type check

Produces: the variety the task-specific clarification asks for.

  1. Mark each test as measurement, observation, opinion or laboratory.
  2. If every row says the same, you have one method, not various.
  3. Aim for at least three different kinds across the specification.
Step 4

Build the success criteria

2 tools

Four-threshold scale

Produces: the rubric both samples built.

  1. Take one measure per aspect — games included, protein grams, cost.
  2. Set the 7–8 value first: what does excellent actually look like?
  3. Work down in even steps to 1–2. Keep the same measure at every band.

MoSCoW

Produces: a priority order, so Criterion D evaluates the important things first.

  1. Sort every criterion into Must, Should, Could, Won’t.
  2. Cap the Musts — if everything is a must, nothing is.
  3. Evaluate Musts first when you reach Criterion D.

Watch for

Four traps

Justification that is really opinion

“The environment is extremely important for all of us and hence this product should be eco-friendly.”

True, but it comes from nowhere. Every justification should point at a survey, a source or a product you analysed.

No testing method

A specification table with only description and justification.

Criterion D evaluates against this strand. A criterion with no named test cannot be evaluated later.

Success criteria with no numbers

“7–8: the product looks good. 1–2: the product does not look good.”

Find the measure. Games included, grams of protein, cost in rupees, seconds to assemble.

Specification written after the design

Students design first, then reverse-engineer specifications to match.

The specification is the brief for the design. Write it before you sketch anything.
