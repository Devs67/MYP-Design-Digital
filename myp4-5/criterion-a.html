Criterion A — Inquiring and analysing

What must be in each strand, the command term at every band, and a worked example.

Strand i — objective Explain and justify the need for a solution to a problem for a specified target audience.

Command terms — Criterion A, strand i

1–2StateGive a specific name, value or other brief answer without explanation or calculation.
3–4OutlineGive a brief account or summary.
5–6ExplainGive a detailed account including reasons or causes.
7–8JustifyGive valid reasons or evidence to support an answer or conclusion.

Explaining is done with reasoning. Justifying cannot be done without evidence.

The flow

Four steps, in this order

Steps 1 to 3 reach 5–6. Step 4 is the one that reaches 7–8.

Step 1

Establish the context

Set the problem in its wider world.

Step 2

Identify the users

Name the specific target audience.

Step 3

Present cause and reason

Use a tool, then write the problem statement.

Step 4

Justify the need

Cite 10–12 secondary sources and primary research.

Step 4 cannot be done from your desk. It needs research gathered before you write.

In detail

What goes into each step

Each step includes an extract from a strong response, and what that extract is doing.

Step 1

Establish the context

  • Explain the problem situation within its wider setting
  • Frame it through the unit's key concept, related concepts and global context
  • Move from the wide picture down to your own setting
Key concept, related concepts and global context: the context must be framed through these from 5–6 upwards. At 7–8 each one must be explicitly identified and labelled as a key concept, a related concept or the global context. Which ones apply to this unit will be introduced in class — leave room for them here.

Extract from a strong response

"The worlds' largest democracy faces a grave problem, one that may not show its symptoms now, but will come to wreak havoc on its growth plans in the decades to come… However, the Indian Government and multiple NGOs have moved swiftly to try and tackle this problem through policy initiatives and public awareness campaigns."

What it does: opens at national scale, names the community affected, then ends on what has changed recently — parents becoming more willing to invest. That last move is what makes the problem look solvable rather than simply sad.

Step 2

Identify the target audience

  • Name the specific target audience or user group
  • Describe who they actually are — age, setting, routine, needs
  • Say why this group and not a wider or narrower one
  • Show evidence that you interacted with them
  • State your conclusion from that interaction here, and cite the full evidence in the appendix
  • An empathy map can be included here as well
Evidence of interaction Interview notes · Survey responses · Photographs · Observation records · Empathy map

Put the raw evidence in the appendix and write only the conclusion here. The appendix number goes in the text: (Appendix B, 2.1).
Three things to understand User — who they are and what they want
Task — what they are trying to do
Environment — where and when they are doing it

A solution has to work for all three. Most students describe only the user.
Attitude and anthropometrics are the two most students skip. Attitude explains why a user avoids the current solution. Anthropometrics turns "it should be comfortable" into a measurement you can design to.

Extract from a strong response

"…the problem must be addressed from the root, by targeting those who will have the maximum upside in the future — children aged 10 to 14. These years are critical for cognitive development and attaining physical maturity."

What it does: does not just name an age group, it argues for it. The reasoning about growth and cognitive development is what turns a stated audience into an identified one.

Step 3

Present the cause and reason, then state the problem

  • Critically present the cause and the reason behind the problem
  • Show it through a graphic organiser, not prose alone
  • Include the perspectives of everyone involved
  • Write the conclusions and insights from your secondary research that are relevant to the cause
  • This can be presented as an infographic
  • Create the problem statement
Tools Statistical analysis · Empathy map · Circle of Viewpoints · Infographic

Only the relevant findings belong here. Secondary research that does not explain the cause stays in the appendix.

Extract from a strong response

"This diet is the result of years of poverty, carbohydrates like wheat and rice are filling, easily replenishing, and cheap, making them a large portion of the diet."

What it does: gives a cause with a reason behind it, rather than restating the problem. The response then sets out three separate perspectives — the snacking brands, the children, and the parents — which is a Circle of Viewpoints written as prose.

What it is missing: the perspectives are written out but never shown as an organiser. The TSC asks for a graphic organiser, so the empathy map or viewpoints circle needs to appear as a visual too.

Step 4

Justify the need

  • Justify the need for a solution to the problem
  • Cite a minimum of 10–12 secondary sources
  • Include relevant primary research
  • After every statistic, say what it means for your users
Primary research — choose what fits Survey · Interview · Observation · Focus group discussion · Expert opinion or view

More than one method is stronger than one done well. A number from a survey and a quote from an interview prove different things.

Extract from a strong response

"A comprehensive meta-analysis, spanning over two decades has placed the childhood obesity rate in India at 8.4%, while overweight prevalence stands at 12.4%… These are grave indicators of upcoming health issues that will plague Indian society if not tackled in a timely manner."

What it does: a statistic followed immediately by what it means. Data alone would sit at 5–6; the sentence after it is what justifies.

What it is missing: the citation is only "Refer Appendix C". A source with no name and no year is not a citation. Write it as: source name, year, appendix number.

Worked example

A complete strand i response

Reproduced in full and unedited. The section headings are the student's own — the step tags show which part of the strand each one covers.

Read the complete responseApprox. 1,400 words · 4 sections

Unit context: child nutrition in India. Outcome: a savoury snack for children aged 10 to 14.

Context

Step 1

The worlds' largest democracy faces a grave problem, one that may not show its symptoms now, but will come to wreak havoc on its growth plans in the decades to come — rampant child malnutrition and anemia. India happens to be the nation with the most vegetarians whose diets are dominated by carbohydrates, reinforced with little protein or fibre as they tend to be more expensive than carbs such as wheat or rice. Thus, the nation finds itself with a population deficient in protein, iron and fibre. To further aggravate this problem, it also finds its supermarkets overflowing with cheap, processed and unhealthy snacks which are rapidly consumed by the ever growing population of children in the nation. A large majority of Indian men and women find themselves engulfed in the daily pressures of work, forcing them to turn a blind-eye to their children's consumption of such processed and unhealthy garbage simply for the sake of convenience. However, the Indian Government and multiple NGOs have moved swiftly to try and tackle this problem through policy initiatives and public awareness campaigns targeted towards parents, shining a light on what it means to truly be healthy, and the ways to accomplish that. As a result of such initiatives, parents' understanding and willingness to invest in their children's nutritional requirements has been growing, particularly the cities of India, across all 3 tiers.

Why this works: it opens at national scale, names the communities affected, and closes on what has changed recently. That final move — parents becoming more willing to invest — is what makes the problem look solvable rather than simply sad.

Target Audience

Step 2

Having considered the various factors surrounding the nutrition issue in India, it is of paramount importance to solve the many issues plaguing its young and ever growing population. In order to do this, identifying a target audience, an age group or demographic will help build a solution specifically catered to their needs, helping prevent excess expenditure while providing maximum reach. This process hinges upon a greater understanding of the impacts of iron, protein and fibre deficiencies in the long-term. In brief, prolonged iron deficiency can cause cognitive impairment, a reduced ability to function, grasp knowledge and adapt to different environments while fibre deficiencies cause digestive issues, often resulting in weight gain. At the same time, protein deficiencies in adults can go fairly unnoticed apart from situations wherein they exert themselves however, a protein deficiency as a growing child, above the age of 10, would cause problems that could last a lifetime. These problems include stunted growth, reduced physical fitness and a noticeable degradation in the performance of the immune system. Together, they create a person bound for a lifetime of hardship. It is due to this reason, the problem must be addressed from the root, by targeting those who will have the maximum upside in the future — children aged 10 to 14. These years are critical for cognitive development and attaining physical maturity, during which providing the most balanced, all-rounded and consistent nutrition will allow them to reach their full potential.

Why this works: it does not just name an age group, it argues for it. The reasoning about growth and cognitive development is what turns a stated audience into an identified one.

What is missing: no evidence of interaction with the audience. There is no interview, survey or empathy map, and no conclusion drawn from meeting a real user. That evidence belongs here, with the raw material cited to the appendix.

Problem

Step 3

India is a nation known for its cultural diversity, but in this diversity, its food culture has one aspect in common — the prevalence of carbohydrates in their diet coupled with minimal consumption of protein and fibres. This diet is the result of years of poverty, carbohydrates like wheat and rice are filling, easily replenishing, and cheap, making them a large portion of the diet. 39% of India is vegetarian, a diet infamous for its protein deficiency. These underlying cultural developments have led to the problem of malnutrition in India.

Moreover, the introduction of snacks such as chips, wafers and crisps to the Indian market has led to the compounding of this malnutrition problem. These snacks have become more prevalent in the Indian market in recent years, and to understand the reasons for consumption, and how it is changing society, it is essential to analyse the various perspectives of all those involved — namely the parents of adolescent children, the adolescents themselves and the large snacking brands in the Indian market. (Refer Appendix C for study)

Snacking brands have only one goal — to turn a profit and ensure investor satisfaction. In order to achieve this target, these brands aim to create flavourful and almost addictive products which primarily appeal to children and adolescents. Across years of product development, these snacks have consistently progressed towards becoming unhealthier for the consumer, in part due to the discovery of flavour and colour enhancers but also the lack of regulation on nutritional requirements for snacks. It has become increasingly convenient to load new flavours with enhancers such as MSG (monosodium glutamate), artificial flavours and artificial colours like RED-40. The vibrant and prominent colours attract children, while artificial flavours and flavour enhancers hook them onto these flavours, making them seek out every opportunity to purchase packs. In order to produce these snacks in a cheap and time efficient manner, manufacturers began sourcing low quality ingredients and using cheap oils such as palm oil in their products. This unhealthy combination of low-quality ingredients, unhealthy oils, artificial flavours, colours and enhancers create a deadly blend. RED-40, a dye derived from petroleum is safe in small doses, but can trigger an inflammatory response in the colon and rectum, causing discomfort for the consumer. Although, some studies have linked the consumption of this dye to the development of migraines, mental disorders and cognitive impairment in regular consumers. Such findings have prompted many nations to ban the sale and use of this dye, but in India, its use remains legal with generous limits being set by the FSSAI. The risk posed by RED-40 pales in comparison to the risks associated with the repeated consumption of palm oil. Increased consumption of palm oil increases the amount of LDL Cholesterol (bad cholesterol) in the bloodstream, increasing the risk of cardiovascular disease while also contributing to rampant weight gain. (reference Appendix C)

However, from the perspective of children, who largely cannot comprehend the long term effects of the regular consumption of such snacks, they remain simply too “tasty”, “crunchy” and “delicious” to let go of. Those who do understand the long term effects and are willing to make significant changes to their diet, the obstacle of bland, ‘weird’ tasting health foods still remains. On the other hand, parents often find themselves purchasing such snacks as the last resort, knowing the risks associated. Many parents find themselves preoccupied with work, stressed and incredibly tired to provide home-made, nutritious snacking options. Few parents try to reason with their children, asking them to consume the more nutritious snacks, but these children often resist, prompting them to purchase conventional unhealthy snacks.

Why this works: the cause comes with a reason behind it rather than restating the problem, and the secondary research on RED-40 and palm oil is used to explain why the snacks are harmful. Three perspectives are set out — brands, children, parents — which is a Circle of Viewpoints written as prose.

What is missing: the perspectives never appear as a graphic organiser, and the secondary research insights are not summarised as an infographic. The task-specific clarification asks for a visual, so prose alone does not meet it however well written.

The Need

Step 4

The consequences of consuming such unhealthy snacks and maintaining an unbalanced diet have never been more grave. Studies indicate growing levels of childhood obesity, childhood diabetes, and stunted growth in Indian society over the past 10 years. A comprehensive meta-analysis, spanning over two decades has placed the childhood obesity rate in India at 8.4%, while overweight prevalence stands at 12.4%. This same study estimated annual spending amounting to USD $780 million to treat and reduce childhood obesity. India has also witnessed a sharp rise in childhood diabetes cases, recording more than 10 cases per 100,000 people in urban areas. Chennai, a large metropolitan city, recorded an increase in cases, currently at 10.5 cases per 100,000 residents, peaking at ages between 10 and 12. This growing nation has also recorded a historic increase in cardiovascular disease, a study conducted in 2021 reported India had the highest number of childhood cardiovascular disease (CVD) cases, standing at 261,465, noting an increase in childhood CVD incidence by 1.28% annually. These are grave indicators of upcoming health issues that will plague Indian society if not tackled in a timely manner. (Refer to Appendix C)

Thus, it is necessary to create a snack, which can replace or reduce the regular consumption of ultra-processed, artificial junk by the target age group of 10 to 14 year old children in order to pave the way for a lifetime of healthy living, cutting short the deficiencies and minimising the long-term impact of malnutrition. These snacks are the most convenient mode of nutrition delivery — being easy to manufacture at large volumes, shelf-stable for months together, and most importantly, will be the path of least resistance for the target group. It is also rather convenient that the Indian market has few competitors in the healthy savoury snack category, with most market players focussing on protein bars or more traditionally ‘healthy’ and ‘organic’ produce such as unprocessed nuts, oats, cereals, etc. Even within this small group of savoury healthy snack brands, no brand markets themselves towards children. Instead they choose to attract the athletes, the gym enthusiasts or the health-conscious adults. This rare situation makes the market the most receptive it may ever be towards a ‘savory snack’ specially created for children aged between 10 and 14.

However, the need is not only for a savoury snack for the young, it is for a snack that is:

  • Rich in iron to reduce the risk of anemia
  • Rich in fibre to reduce gut problems and ensure natural weight reduction
  • High in protein to support muscle growth and overall bodily development and function in arguably the most critical stage of a human's life
  • Low in fats to prevent excess calorie intake and reduce the risk of weight gain
  • Balanced carbohydrate quantity to provide much needed energy
  • Affordably priced, allowing for a wide range of consumers to purchase and reap the benefits
  • Flavourful, attracting the target group by providing flavours of their liking without increasing the amount of sugar or salt content
  • Attractively packaged to grab attention and encourage the young to try out this new innovation

Why this works: each statistic is followed by what it means, and the section closes on the market gap — no brand targets children. That gap is the justification. The closing list stays on the right side of the line: these are needs, not specifications, because none of them carries a number.

What is missing: two things. No primary research at all — no survey, interview, observation or focus group with children aged 10 to 14. And the citations are only “Refer Appendix C”, which names no source and no year. Both are required for 7–8.

Thinking tools

Frameworks for each step

Tools marked named in TSC appear in the task-specific clarification itself. The others are optional, but each one produces something a step needs.

Step 1

Establish the context

3 tools

Think · Puzzle · Explore

Produces: the opening context, and your first research questions.

  1. What do I think I know about this situation?
  2. What puzzles me about it?
  3. How could I explore the puzzles? Each puzzle becomes a research question for strand ii.

Scale ladder

Produces: the wide-to-narrow movement a strong context needs.

  1. Find evidence at global, national, state and school level.
  2. Open your writing at the widest rung where evidence holds.
  3. Narrow down, rung by rung, until you reach your own setting.

Concept mapping

Produces: the link between your problem and the unit's concepts.

  1. Write the problem in the centre.
  2. Branch out to the key concept, the related concepts and the global context.
  3. On each branch, write one sentence connecting the concept to your problem. Those sentences become your context paragraph.
Step 2

Identify the target audience

5 tools

User profiling

Produces: an audience description with depth rather than a list of ages.

  1. Demographic — age, gender, job, family size.
  2. Motivation and needs — why are they doing this task, and what is their goal?
  3. Culture, beliefs and values — what do they hold that is relevant to this task?
  4. Attitude — how do they perceive the task? Do they like it, hate it, look forward to it, feel embarrassed or frustrated by it?
  5. Anthropometric and human factors — are they in the 5th, 50th or 95th percentile? What physical limitations do they have in completing the task?

Empathy map

Named in TSC

Produces: a four-sided picture of your user, and the quotes you need.

  1. Four quadrants: says, thinks, does, feels.
  2. Fill "says" and "does" from interviews and observation only — never from imagination.
  3. Fill "thinks" and "feels" from what you infer, and mark those as inferences.

Stakeholder map

Produces: the difference between who suffers and who decides.

  1. Primary user — who lives with the problem daily.
  2. Secondary user — who else is affected by it.
  3. Decision maker — who can approve a change.

User persona

Produces: the hobbies, likes and dislikes the strand asks for.

  1. Build one representative person: name, age, year group, routine.
  2. Add interests, dislikes, and where they encounter the problem.
  3. Base every detail on a real person you spoke to, not a stereotype.

User journey

Produces: the moment and the place the problem bites.

  1. Map your user's day as a timeline of steps.
  2. Mark where the problem appears.
  3. The marked point tells you where a solution has to work.
Step 3

Present the cause and reason

6 tools

Circle of Viewpoints

Named in TSC

Produces: the multiple perspectives that make the analysis critical.

  1. List every group involved — usually three or four.
  2. For each, write: I am thinking of this from the viewpoint of… I think… A question I have is…
  3. Show it as a circle diagram, not only as paragraphs.

Statistical analysis

Named in TSC

Produces: your own data made visible.

  1. Turn your survey, tally or observation into a chart.
  2. Choose the chart for the job — bar for comparison, line for change over time, pie only for parts of one whole.
  3. Write one sentence under it saying what the chart shows. A chart with no sentence scores nothing.

Fishbone

Produces: a wide search for causes before you commit to one.

  1. Problem in the head, six bones: people, process, place, tools, time, rules.
  2. Three causes per bone — the quota forces breadth.
  3. Mark each cause as evidenced or guessed.

Five Whys

Produces: the reason behind the cause, which is what "critically present" asks for.

  1. Start at the symptom and ask why, five times.
  2. Stop when the next why leaves your control.
  3. Any why you cannot evidence becomes a research question.

Problem tree

Produces: proof you are designing for a cause and not a symptom.

  1. Roots are causes, trunk is the core problem, branches are effects.
  2. Place your current statement on the tree.
  3. If it sits in the branches, move down to the trunk and rewrite.

Problem statement frame

Produces: the problem statement itself, in one sentence.

  1. [Target audience] needs a way to [do what] because [evidenced reason].
  2. Check it names no product.
  3. Check every part traces back to something on this page.
Step 4

Justify the need

4 tools

Rule of three

Produces: justification that cannot be dismissed as one person's opinion.

  1. Every claim needs a number, a voice and an observation.
  2. One source is an anecdote; two of the same kind repeat one blind spot.
  3. Three different kinds of evidence cannot all be wrong in the same direction.

Source card

Produces: the 10–12 citations, recorded as you go rather than reconstructed at the end.

  1. One card per source: where from, when, who it studied, what it says.
  2. Add the appendix number the moment you file it.
  3. No card means no citation, and no citation means no justification.

OPVL

Produces: the judgement of sources that turns collecting into analysing.

  1. Origin — who made it, when, what kind of source.
  2. Purpose — why it was made, and for whom.
  3. Value and Limitation — both must follow from the origin and purpose.

PEEL

Produces: paragraphs where the claim and its proof sit together.

  1. Point — the claim, in one sentence.
  2. Evidence — the statistic or quote, with its source.
  3. Explain, then Link back to your target audience.

Watch for

Four traps

Naming the product

"I will design an app to fix this."

This strand is about the need. Write what a solution must do, never what it will be.

The invisible user

"This is a problem for everyone."

Name the group, describe them, and say why them. If you cannot picture one real person, you have not identified an audience.

Organiser missing

Perspectives written as paragraphs but never shown as a visual.

The TSC asks for a graphic organiser. Prose alone does not meet it, however well written.

Statistics with no meaning

Paragraphs of numbers with nothing said about them.

After every number, answer one question: what does this mean for my users?

Areas come before questions. Students who start with questions end up with a random list that covers nothing systematically.

In detail

What goes into each step

Each step includes an extract from a strong response, and what that extract is doing.

Step 1

Identify the research areas

  • Break your problem into the areas that need investigating
  • An area is a topic, not a question — nutrition, safety, cost, packaging
  • Show the areas as a mind map or flowchart before you write any questions
  • Every area must trace back to something in strand i
Tools Mind map · Flowchart · Table

ACCESS FM works well as an area generator: aesthetics, cost, customer, environment, size, safety, function, materials. Pick the headings that apply to your problem.

From a strong response

Research Plan → Secondary Sources → Sources: reports on snack consumption habits in India; studies on child nutrition and dietary needs; articles on food processing and preservation methods.

What it does: the mind map splits into primary and secondary first, then each branch splits again into sources and purpose. Naming the purpose beside every source is what makes it a plan rather than a list.

Step 2

Construct the research questions

  • Write questions under each research area
  • Minimum of five questions across the plan
  • Each question must be answerable by a real source you can reach
  • Avoid questions that can be answered yes or no

From a strong response

“What cooking process would be the most effective at maintaining nutritional quantities and using the minimum fat?”

What it does: it is specific enough to research and open enough to produce a finding that changes the design. Compare it with a weak version — “is cooking important?” — which can be answered without research and changes nothing.

Step 3

Identify the type of research

  • Mark every question as primary or secondary
  • Name the actual source, not just the category
  • Cite it to the appendix or bibliography
  • A question can use both types — say so
Primary methods Survey · Interview · Observation · Focus group discussion · Expert opinion · Existing product testing
Secondary sources Journal articles, books, magazines — useful for learning about a topic, users, or a design opportunity. These offer a more in-depth and focused perspective.
Example: reading a journal article to learn about the technical aspects of a design opportunity.

Databases — collections of data, digital or physical. Useful for gathering ergonomic or statistical data.
Example: using a national database of ergonomic data to determine the leg length of the user group.

News reports, documentaries, videos — useful for learning about the current state of an issue, and how people, the economy and the environment are being affected.
Example: watching a news report on rising ocean levels and how this is impacting a local community.

Citations — use citations for all your secondary research: images, charts and infographics as well as articles and databases. Citations can appear at the bottom of the page or next to the work.

Notice what is not on this list: a general web search. “Website” as a source tells a reader nothing. Name the journal, the database, the documentary or the report.

From a strong response

Primary — Ms Dhara Mavuleti, a renowned nutritionist, specialising in the needs of adolescent children. (Refer Appendix A1)

What it does: names a real person, states her expertise, and points to where the interview is stored. “Primary — interview” on its own tells the reader nothing about whether the source is any good.

Watch for: the same response uses “Website (Refer Appendix C)” for five different questions. If five questions share one source label, either name the five sites or admit the research is thinner than it looks.

Step 4

Prioritize and justify

  • Give every question a priority
  • State how the research will help you solve the problem
  • Not everything can be high — priority means some things matter less
  • The justification column is the one most students leave out
Two ways to show priority High / Medium / Low — quick, readable, works for most plans
Ranked numbers — 1 to 12 in order of importance, forces harder decisions

From a strong response

“The average size of the board can make sure that the product I aim to create is not too small or too big. This product should include the regular board games in a space saving manner.”

What it does: connects the research directly to a design decision. That is what “stated how the research will help you solve the problem” is asking for — not why the topic is interesting, but what you will do differently once you know the answer.

Question stems

Produces: open questions that generate findings.

  1. Which… / What… / How… are safe openings.

  3. Test each one: could two different sources give different answers? If not, it is not a research question.

Produces: the justification column, written as you go.

  1. Answer your own question with a guess.
  2. Ask: what would I do differently if that were the answer?
  3. If nothing would change, delete the question. If something would, that sentence is your justification.

Identify the research type

2 tools

Method chooser

Produces: the right method for each question rather than a survey for everything.

MoSCoW

Impact and effort grid

Produces: a realistic plan for a school timetable.

  1. Plot each question: how much it changes the design against how hard it is to answer.
  2. High impact and low effort goes first.

In detail

What goes into each step

Each step includes an extract from a strong response, and what that extract is doing.

Step 1

Choose a diverse range of products

  • Pick products that already try to solve a similar problem
  • Range means genuinely different, not five versions of the same thing
  • Include a clear colour picture of every product
  • Number depends on the band you are aiming for

From a strong response

“I have chosen 4 different existing products that all fall under the same category that combine board games and bring people together over said games.”

What it does: states the selection rule up front. The products share a purpose but differ in form — a game house, a double-sided board, a games table, a folding wooden set. That is what a range looks like.

Step 2

Set your aspects for analysis

  • Decide the lens before you look at any product
  • Use the same aspects for every product so they can be compared
  • Define each aspect in a sentence — what exactly are you judging?
  • Six aspects is a workable number
Tools ACCESS FM · Parts–Purpose–Complexities · Product Matrix

Or build your own aspects from your specification, as long as you define each one.

From a strong response

“Taste and Texture — feedback from longtime consumers to reflect on the flavour profile of the product, mouth feel (crispy, crunchy, etc).”

What it does: the aspect is not just named, it is defined. A reader knows exactly what will be judged before any product appears, which is what makes the six analyses comparable.

Step 3

Analyse each product

  • Good points and bad points against every aspect
  • Annotate the picture itself with callouts where you can
  • Analyse, do not describe — say what the feature achieves or fails to achieve
  • Every product needs weaknesses. A product with none has not been analysed

From a strong response

“This plastic on the board prevents damage to dust, settling on the cardboard or any other food item that might destroy the game. And hence it is durable.”  /  “This game does include sharp corners and the plastic can create sharper edges. And hence this game is not extremely safe for children under the age of 5.”

What it does: the same material is judged twice under the same aspect — a strength for durability, a weakness for safety. That double reading is analysis. A description would just say “the board is covered in plastic”.

Step 4

Justify how the analysis helped

  • Say what you will carry forward into your own solution
  • Say what you will deliberately avoid, and why
  • Connect each finding to your problem, not to the product
  • Cite every picture and every piece of copied text

From a strong response

“While the product is functional and portable, it lacks game piece storage and may take up more space than expected. Furthermore, there are some safety concerns regarding sharp corners and plastic edges.”

What it does: a closing paragraph per product that gathers the analysis into a judgement. The missing storage becomes a requirement for the student's own design, which is exactly the justification the checklist asks for.

Design question frame Phrase it as “How might we…”. It keeps the brief open to more than one solution.

Example: “How might we improve the storage of shoes and jackets in order to make the entrance of the home organized and accessible for residents?”

Step 2

Describe the outcome

  • Say what will be produced, and how
  • Then say how the solution will be used
  • Keep the two parts separate — what it is, then what it is for

2a — what will be produced, and how

“A scale model of a storage and organization system for the entrance will be produced. This model will be produced using Fusion 360, the laser cutter, and cardboard sheet that are assembled by hand.”

2b — how the solution will be used

“This model will be used to explore the form and function, and also gather feedback from my client and expert.”

Splitting the outcome in two stops students describing the object and forgetting to say what it is for.

This answer ranged from 2 all the way to 19. The best way to collect data will be finding the most common answer and stem my research from there. Based on the information in the survey, families with 3–4 members were repeatedly mentioned. This helped me understand that ideally these games should be 2–4 player games to include multiple people and bring several people together. This product should not include one player games as that defeats the purpose of bringing communities together with board games.

In this case, the survey revealed information about popular board games such as Ticket to Ride and Codenames that I was not previously aware of. As a result, the designer decided to conduct additional research on these games in order to better understand their gameplay mechanics and design elements. And further analyse the relevance of these games in the product that needs to be designed.

Why this works: the survey response is summarised, not reproduced. The raw data sits in Appendix A.2 and only the conclusion and the design decision appear here. Note the footnote reference — that is what citing looks like on this page.

What is weaker: “the designer decided” switches to third person mid-paragraph. Small, but a brief written in first person should stay in first person.

Thinking tools

Frameworks for each step

Step 1

State what you will make

1 tool

The design statement frame

Produces: the opening line, in the exact wording the task sheet asks for.

  1. I am going to design and make a [product]…
  2. …for [target audience]…
  3. …so that [what it lets them do].
Step 2

Who, where and why

1 tool

Who · Where · Why check

Produces: three separate checklist ticks in three sentences.

  1. Who — the target audience by name, carried from strand i.
  2. Where — a real, physical place the product will be used.
  3. Why — the reason this audience, not a wider one.
Step 3

Summarise the research

2 tools

Strand summary grid

Produces: the summary paragraphs, one row at a time.

  1. One row per strand: what I found → what it means for my design.
  2. Write the right-hand column first — it is the part that matters.
  3. Turn each row into one or two sentences of prose.

The relevance filter

Produces: a brief that fits on one page.

  1. For each finding, ask: did this change my design?
  2. If yes, summarise it and name the change.
  3. If no, leave it in the appendix. It is research, but it is not relevant research.
Step 4

Question, features and SOI

3 tools

Design question frame

Produces: the one element that only appears at 7–8.

  1. How can I design a [product] that [does what] for [audience]?
  2. Check it can be answered by a thing you could actually build.
  3. Check it can be tested in Criterion D.

Needs to features

Produces: the key features, traced to evidence.

  1. List the needs your research established.
  2. Turn each into a feature the product will have.
  3. Keep numbers out — measurements belong in the Criterion B specification.

SOI bridge

Produces: the connection the task-specific clarification asks for at every band above 1–2.

  1. Write the statement of inquiry out in full.
  2. Underline the part your solution acts on.
  3. Write one sentence linking your product to that underlined idea.

Quality

Watch for

Four traps

Never saying what it is

A whole page about the need, with no product named.

Strand i forbids naming a product. Strand iv requires it in the first line. The rule flips here.

Repeating strand i

The problem explained again from scratch, at the same length.

Summarize means abstract the major points. One page, not a second version of the whole investigation.

The missing design question

Everything else present, no question stated.

It is required at 7–8 and at no other band. One sentence, and it is the cheapest mark on this page.

Specification creep

“It shall be 40cm by 40cm and hold twelve games.”

Key features stay broad in the brief. Numbers arrive in Criterion B.
