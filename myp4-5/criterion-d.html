Step 1

Start from a specification and its target

  • Open your Criterion B specification table. Every test starts there.
  • Write the specification’s name or number exactly as in Criterion B.
  • Write the target: the result that counts as success, as a number with its unit. It must match your Criterion B success criteria.
Weak targetStrong target
Pages load fastEach page loads in under 3 seconds
Easy to navigateAt least 80% of users reach the Support page in 3 clicks or fewer
Few mistakesFewer than 2 broken links across the whole site

Rule: no number, no target. No target, no way to say met or not met.

From Sori, Test 1 “At least 80% of the target audience must rate the interface as visually appealing”

A target with a number, but it does not say which answers count as “appealing”. The guide makes it exact: “at least 80% of respondents rate each aspect 4 or 5 out of 5”.

How to choose: what is my specification really asking?

If your specification asks…UseYou get
Can users do something?User trial and observationTime, clicks, success or not
What do users think or feel?Survey, then interviewRatings and quotes
Does the site work?Performance testMeasurements, pass or fail
Is the content right?Expert appraisalA checklist score and comments

Tip: numbers tell you whether a specification was met. Words tell you why. A strong test often uses both.

Step 3

Write who, how many and the steps

  • Who: people from your target audience, teens aged 13–16. Not only friends who watched you build it.
  • How many: at least 5 users for a user test. For a performance test, test every page, or repeat each check 3 times.
  • Write it in full: “5 students aged 13–16 who have not used the site before, 1 trial each.”
  • From your template: a single peer survey with a few responses is not enough.

Steps another person could follow

Weak

Ask users to find the help page and time them.

Strong

1. Open the home page on a laptop, full screen.
2. Read the task card aloud: “You want to talk to someone about exam stress. Find where to go.”
3. Start the stopwatch at the first click.
4. Stop it when the Support page opens. Give no hints.
5. Record the seconds, the number of clicks and whether the user finished.
6. Clear the browser history before the next user.

Analysis techniques, with a quick sum

TechniqueUse it when you haveQuick sumSentence for your analysis line
MeanSeveral numbers, such as times22, 18, 30, 25, 15 s → 110 ÷ 5 = 22 s“I will calculate the mean ___ from ___ users and compare it with my target of ___.”
ModeRatings4, 5, 4, 3, 4 → most common = 4“I will find the most common rating out of ___.”
Percentage of success or failureYes or no results4 of 5 finished → 4 ÷ 5 × 100 = 80%“I will calculate the percentage of users who ___, and compare it with my target of ___%.”
Comparison against the targetAny result and its targetMean 22 s, target under 30 s → met“If the result is ___ or better, the specification is met.”
Frequency countWords or events to tally3 of 5 users said “the menu is confusing” → 3“I will sort users’ comments into themes and count how many mention each one.”
Error rateErrors out of attempts2 broken links out of 40 → 2 ÷ 40 × 100 = 5%“I will count the ___ out of ___ and turn it into a percentage.”
Trend or patternResults over several daysVisits each day: 2, 3, 3, 5, 4, 6, 7 → rising“I will record ___ every day for ___ days and describe how it changes.”

Match the analysis to the data. Times → mean, then compare with the target · yes or no → percentage of success · ratings → mean and mode · mistakes → error rate · quotes → frequency count · results over days → trend or pattern.

In detail

What goes into each step

Every example is from Sori, the app in the worked-example guide. Try the sums yourself before you open the answers.

Step 1

Put the raw data in the appendix

  • Place the completed tool for each test in Appendix D(ii).1 to D(ii).5, and cite it in the table.
  • Summarise the data in the table with units: how many people, what they rated, how many comments.

Sori Test 2, the raw data

User’s ageHow it was recordedRating out of 5
13Audio recording, written out4.5
16Written feedback4.5 (and 9 out of 10 for user experience)
35Message4
45Message, passed on by the 35-year-old4
Add up the ratings before you read onShow answer
4.5 + 4.5 + 4 + 4 = 17 out of 20. The portfolio says 19.

Step 2

Do the sum you named in strand i

  • Use the technique from your strand i analysis line. Write the working, not only the answer.
  • Mean from a rating table: (each rating × how many people gave it, added up) ÷ number of people. Example, Sori colour scheme: (3×2 + 4×12 + 5×17) ÷ 31 = 139 ÷ 31 = 4.48.
  • Check every total against the appendix. A claim must match its evidence.
From Sori, Test 1 “96.9% of the respondents find the app visually pleasing.”

Small, but nobody can check it without the working. 30 ÷ 31 × 100 = 96.8%.

Step 3

Count the words

  • Sort written comments into themes and count each one (frequency count).
  • The numbers say whether a specification was met. The words say why, and what to improve. These counts are your evidence for strand iii.
  • Example, Sori Test 2: navigation or layout needs work, 3 of 4 users; too slow or not smooth, 2 of 4; calm colours or nice look, 3 of 4.

Step 4

Judge it critically

  • Give the level from your Criterion B success criteria: met, partly met or not met (or your 3/2/1 level).
  • Say by how much: “16.8 percentage points above the 80% target.”
  • Name the weakest aspect, even when the test passed.
  • Say how far the evidence can be trusted: how many people, were they your target audience, did you watch them.
From Sori, Test 2 “A single target user conducted the usability test”

A 9 out of 10 from one person, reported as an average. Say it comes from one person, or test more.

Evaluate your results

Analysis calculator

Pick the technique you named in strand i and type in your own results. You get the answer, the working, and a sentence in the order result → working → target → met or not met. Copy the working too: a number without its working cannot be checked.

Mean

Add all the results, then divide by how many there are.

Use this when a survey tells you how many people gave each rating, as Google Forms does.

Mode

The result that comes up most often. Works for numbers and for answers such as yes, no, maybe.

Percentage of success or failure

How many passed, divided by how many tried, × 100.

Comparison against the target

Is your result better or worse than the number you set in Criterion B? And by how much?

Frequency count

Sort what people said into themes, then count how many mention each one.

One person can mention more than one theme, so the counts can add up to more than the number of people. To remove a theme, clear its name.

Error rate

Errors divided by attempts, × 100.

Trend or pattern

How results change over days. Use it for your week-long trial in strand iv.

Means are rounded to 2 decimal places and percentages to 1. Show the same rounding in your template.

Test 2. Design specifications: Target User and User Experience.
Targets (Criterion B success criteria): Target User “should receive approximately 15/20 or more”; User Experience “approximately 7/10 or higher in the usability test”.		 Data: 4 users aged 13, 16, 35 and 45, each rating the app out of 5 (4.5, 4.5, 4, 4); the 16-year-old also rated user experience 9 out of 10; comments from all 4.

Evidence: Appendix N (in your template: Appendix D(ii).2).		 Level: Target User met; User Experience met, from one user only.

In detail

What goes into each step

Extracts are from a healthcare web app built in HTML, CSS and PHP.

Step 1

Start from the evidence

  • Your strand ii frequency counts are the starting point. In the guide, both Sori tests point to the same two limitations: colour choice (7 of 30 suggestions) and navigation (3 of 4 users).
  • Quote the result or feedback, with its test number or appendix label. “It worked well” is not evidence.
From the healthcare web app · starts from what testers said “users also wished that the layout was improved, as the doctor calls + consultations did not represent the information well”

Step 2

Name the weakness and its cause

  • Say which specification it affects.
  • Give the cause, not the symptom: “the menu label does not say what the section holds”, not “the page was hard to find”.
From the healthcare web app · cause named “users adding their own questionaire + answers was found to not be very efficient, but rather confusing”

Step 3

Propose a specific change

  • Name exactly what you would change: the label, the colour value, the number of menu links, the page.
  • Explain why the change fixes the cause.

Step 4

Say how you would retest it

  • Name the test you would repeat and the target it must now meet.
  • Neither sample does this. It is the easiest place to stand out.

Watch for

Four traps

The wish list

“I could have also incorporated a small game like Wordle.”

If no test or tester pointed to it, it is a new idea, not an improvement.

The symptom, not the cause

“The page was hard to find.”

Say why it was hard to find.

The vague fix

“Make it more user-friendly.”

Name the label, colour, link or page you would change.

No retest

A change with no way to check it worked.

Name the test and the new target.

The flow

Four steps, in this order

The test for this strand: is every impact claim backed by at least a week of real use? The guide says it plainly: tests that happen once tell you “very little” about impact.

Your target audience uses the site for at least seven days.

In detail

What goes into each step

Extracts are from Sori, the app in the worked-example guide.

Step 1

Run a week-long trial

  • The task-specific clarification asks for “at least a week long client/ target audience testing”. Start it as soon as the site works.
  • Give the link to at least 5 teens aged 13–16, the same rule as strand i.
  • Keep a daily log: who visited, which pages, how often. Put the blank log in the appendix with your strand i tools.

Step 2

Measure before, during and after

  • Ask the same rating question on day 1 and day 7: “How sure are you about where to find reliable advice on exam stress? 1–5.” Work out the mean each time.
  • Use trend or pattern analysis on the daily log. Example: visits each day 2, 3, 3, 5, 4, 6, 7 → rising. The analysis calculator in strand ii has a trend tool.
  • Collect at least one direct quote, with who said it and when.

Step 3

Explain impact at three levels

  • Direct: what changed for a user, with the before and after.
  • Audience: did behaviour change beyond one person, such as sharing a page or coming back?
  • Long-term: could the site keep working, be updated, be used by the youth wellness organisation?
  • Link each level back to your Criterion A problem.
From Sori · a limitation named “other features such as the diet & medication and GPS were less impactful”

Step 4

Include the limits

  • Say who the site did not help, or what did not change.
  • Cut any claim your data cannot back.
From Sori · a claim with no data “The app solved all their problems”

Steps 1–2

Trial and measurement

1 tool

Before-and-after against the baseline

Produces: a starting number and an ending number for the same measure.

  1. Retrieve the original tally, timing or count from Criterion A.
  2. Repeat that exact measurement with the solution in use.
  3. Report both numbers together, and be honest if nothing moved.
  4. Full entry: Before-and-after against the baseline in the framework index.
Steps 3–4

Explanation and limits

1 tool

What · So what · Now what

Produces: an impact explained, not just described.

  1. What: what changed during the week, with the numbers.
  2. So what: why it matters for the Criterion A problem.
  3. Now what: what this suggests for long-term use, and its limits.
