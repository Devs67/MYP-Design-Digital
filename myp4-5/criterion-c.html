Criterion C — Creating the solution

What must be in each strand, the steps in order, and two real portfolios showing what works and what is missing.

The flow

Four steps, in this order

The test for this strand is simple: could a classmate make your product from this plan alone, without asking you anything?

Step 1

List every step in making order

One row per operation, verb first.

Step 2

Give each step its resources and time

Tools, materials, quantities, minutes.

Step 3

Put the steps on a timeline

A Gantt chart against the lessons you have.

Step 4

Test the plan on a peer

They read it and tell you what is missing.

In detail

What goes into each step

Extracts are from two real portfolios: a wooden board game cube and a baked snack called Snacktive.

Step 1

List every step in making order

  • Write one row per operation, in the order you will do it. Start each row with a verb: measure, cut, file, bake.
  • Group the rows into phases so the plan can be read at a glance.
  • Put the detail in the plan itself: dimensions, quantities, settings. “Follow the recipe in Criterion B” sends your reader to another page.
From Snacktive, task 5 “Place a mixing bowl on a weighing scale and click the ‘tare’ function to reset weight. This would allow the user to measure the quantity added.”

Step 2

Give each step its resources and time

  • For every row, name the tools and materials, with quantities. “Nails” is a material. “24 nails, 38 mm” is a resource someone can collect.
  • Estimate the time per row in minutes, then total it and compare it with the lessons available.
  • Add a safety note and a quality check to each row: how will you know the step worked before moving on?
  • Name the specification point each step serves, by number.

Step 3

Put the steps on a timeline

  • Draw a Gantt chart: steps down the side, lessons or days across the top.
  • The objective asks for efficient use of time. Show what can overlap. Paint drying and designing the printed boards can happen on the same day.
  • Mark anything with a waiting time: drying, soaking, ordering, printing.
From the board game cube The Gantt chart footer reads: Start Date 10th February, End Date 25th February, Total Days Taken 15 Days.

Step 4

Test the plan on a peer

  • Give the plan to a classmate. Ask them to say, step by step, what they would do.
  • Every question they ask is a gap. Write the answer into the plan.
  • Keep a note of what you added after the peer read. It is evidence that the plan is “sufficient for peers to follow”.

The flow

Four steps, in this order

Your teacher was not standing next to you for every cut. The skill only counts if the evidence shows it.

Step 1

Name the making skills

The techniques your product needs.

Step 2

Capture evidence as you work

Dated photos with the tool in use.

Step 3

Say how well, with a measure

Accuracy, finish, consistency.

Step 4

Show the skill improving

What failed, what you changed, the result.

In detail

What goes into each step

Extracts are from two real portfolios: a wooden board game cube and a baked snack called Snacktive.

Step 1

Name the making skills

  • List the techniques used to make the solution: measuring, cutting, joining, finishing, baking, coding, editing.
  • Research and thinking skills belong to Criteria A and B. They can appear, but they do not replace making skills here.
  • Take the list from your strand i plan. Each making step uses at least one skill.

Step 2

Capture evidence as you work

  • Photograph at the start and end of every session. Include your hands or the tool in use.
  • Caption each photo with the date and the technique shown.
  • Keep faces out of the frame, or cover them before the photo goes in the portfolio.
  • A screenshot of a tutorial is evidence that you watched a video. It is not evidence of your skill.

Step 3

Say how well, with a measure

  • For each skill, state the result in a way that can be checked: a tolerance, a count, a setting, a finish you can photograph.
  • Say which tool and which technique, then what you did to keep it accurate.
From the board game cube “This skill was mainly used to measure and mark the parts which need to be put out to create a perfect 15 x 15 inch cube.”

Step 4

Show the skill improving

  • Write up the attempts that failed. Say what went wrong, what you changed and what happened next.
  • A table of trials is clearer than a paragraph: attempt, setting, result, what I changed.
  • End with the setting or method you settled on.
From Snacktive “The initial batches were more overcooked, being excessively hard, possessing depleted nutrition and having a bitter taste due to burnt edges. The next few batches were underbaked, overly soft and crumbly - not ideal for prolonged shelf storage.”

SnacktiveSkill developed · evidence

Dough Handling and Shaping

Crucially, the next skill required to be developed were kneading skills - something which required significant improvement. This skill involved the usage of a rolling pin to press the dough into the desired shape and thickness to create the bar. While this may sound easy, ensuring uniform thickness without cracking or sticking to the surface proved to be challenging. Across many trials, I began to master this technique, rolling small portions of dough into shape to ensure uniform thickness and then moving on to larger portions. A process that helped ease the burden was cooling the dough. Cooler dough tended to be easier to knead and less likely to crack or stick to the counter-top.

Why this works: it names the difficulty, the technique that solved it and a second finding about cooling the dough. That is a skill developing, described by the person who did it.

Baking and Temperature Control

In order to craft the best possible cracker texture, balancing the need to maintain nutrition and maximise shelf life, a series of trials involving changes in temperature and baking times were carried out. I carefully observed the progress of Snacktive at temperatures ranging between 180-220°C. Additionally, various baking times were tried out, between 25 and 40 minutes to identify the ideal baking temperature and time range.

Why this works: real ranges, real failures and named signs of doneness. The evidence photos show the oven display at two different settings.

What is missing: the answer. The final temperature and time are never stated, and there is no table of batches, so the trials cannot be checked. Two of the six skills are research skills. The measuring paragraph describes digital scales while the photo shows a dial scale. No photo shows the rolled dough or a thickness check.

In detail

What goes into each step

Extracts are from two real portfolios: a wooden board game cube and a baked snack called Snacktive.

Step 1

Set the plan beside what happened

  • Copy the step numbers from your strand i plan. Use the same numbers, in the same order.
  • For each step, show two things side by side: what was planned and what you did.
  • If a step was skipped or changed, say so in the row. The reason goes in strand iv.
From Snacktive Each row has “Intended” and “Used” under Resources, and “Intended” and “Completed” under Process.

Step 2

Record each step as you do it

  • Write the time each step really took, next to the time you planned.
  • Add a dated photo for every step.
  • Note anything you needed help with and who helped.
From the board game cube Step 2.2, cutting: time taken 15 minutes, against 15–30 mins in the plan. The student did not make the comparison. You should.

Step 3

Present the finished solution

  • Photograph the finished solution on a plain background: front, back, sides, open and closed.
  • Add a photo that shows its size, next to a ruler or in someone’s hands.
  • For a digital product, show every screen and add a link.

Step 4

Show that it functions

  • List what the solution is meant to do. Take this from the function points in your specification.
  • Show each function working: a photo, a short video link or a simple check with a result.
  • This is a quick check that it works. Full testing with data belongs to Criterion D.

The flow

Four steps, in this order

The objective names two things that can change: the chosen design and the plan. Cover both.

Design changes and plan changes.

Step 2

The drawing beside the photo.

In detail

What goes into each step

Extracts are from two real portfolios: a wooden board game cube and a baked snack called Snacktive.

Step 1

Log every change the day it happens

  • Keep a modification log open while you make. Write the row on the day of the change.
  • Record changes to the design (size, material, feature, recipe) and changes to the plan (order, time, tool, a skipped step).
  • Compare your strand iii log with your strand i plan. Every difference is a change that needs a row.

Step 2

Show before and after

  • Put the Criterion B drawing next to a photo of what you made.
  • Mark the part that changed.
From the board game cube The table has four columns: Sr. no, Before, After, Justification. Before is the design drawing. After is a photo of the made product.

Step 3

Give the cause, with evidence

  • Say what happened that forced the change: a failed test, a measurement, a missing material, feedback from a user.
  • Give the evidence: a number, a photo of the failure, a quote.
  • Name the alternative you rejected and why.
From Snacktive, change 4 “Approximately 4 tablespoons of extra oat flour was required, and in order to counteract the blandness caused by this, extra salt and cumin was needed.”

Step 4

Link it to the specification

  • Name the specification point the change serves, by number.
  • Say what the change did to the product and to your timeline.
  • If a change weakens a specification point, say so. Criterion D will find it anyway.

Worked example

Two strand iv submissions

One is a before-and-after table with pictures. The other is prose that explains cause and effect.

Board game cubeBefore · after · justification · five changes

Change 4: a base block was added

After adding components like hinges, lock pieces and the handles, the cube was no longer able to rotate and have a straight and balanced level.

Why this works: the cause is a real problem found during making, and the before and after pictures show the fix. Another change is just as clear: the star checkers holes were not carved because “carving the multiple holes with precision would have taken days for quality results”.

Change 5: the cube can be sat on

I asked a fellow student to sit on the product … asked multiple different people to sit one by one and the product remained unaffected

Why this works: the student tested the change with real people and reported what happened.

What is missing: no change names a specification point, so the reader cannot tell whether the product moved closer to or further from the specification. All five changes are to the design; changes to the plan, such as time or order, are not covered. The sitting test gives no weight. No rejected alternative is named.

SnacktiveChange · justification · six changes in prose

Change 4: changing the composition to improve texture

During initial tests of the Snacktive dough, the usage of boiled black beans and cooked quinoa caused the dough to become overly soggy, sticky and not capable of maintaining a shape. Even when baked, the texture was more crumbly than crisp - becoming similar to a cookie and not a chip or cracker. As the fundamental goal of this project is to create a viable alternative to traditional snacking options such as potato chips or other baked crisps - this texture would not be capable of meeting this goal. As a result, some change in the composition was necessary.

Why this works: it runs cause, consequence, change, result. The failure is described exactly, it is tied to the goal of the project, and the fix comes with a quantity. The next paragraph reports what the change did to the dough and the baked result.

Thinking tools

Frameworks for each step

All optional, but each one produces something a step needs.

Steps 1–2

Log and show

1 tool

Modification log

Produces: specific reasons, written while you still remember them.

  1. Columns: date, what changed, why, what I rejected, effect on the specification.
  2. Write the row the day the change happens.
  3. Flag any change that weakens a “must” specification point.
  4. Full entry: Modification log in the framework index.
Steps 3–4

Justify and link

2 tools

PEEL

Produces: a justification that proves its claim.

  1. Point → Evidence → Explain → Link to the specification.
  2. Full entry: PEEL in the framework index.

Change sentence

Produces: one complete justification per change.

  1. “I changed [x] to [y] because [what happened, with evidence]. I rejected [alternative] because [reason]. This serves Spec [n] and cost [time].”

Watch for

Four traps

“No changes were made”

Followed by a list of six changes.

Nobody makes a product with no changes. Compare your plan with your log and count them.

Convenience as a reason

“This was a change made to the plan due to the convenience of …”

Easier for you is a description. Say what the change did for the product or the user.

Design changes only

Five changes to the product and none to the timeline.

The objective says “design and plan”. Late steps and swapped tools count.

Reasons from memory

Writing the whole section in the last week.

The reason fades fast. Log the change on the day it happens.
