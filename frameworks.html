Two borrowed frameworks that carry the writing and the judgement everywhere in the cycle.

PEEL

From English Every paragraph

Point · Evidence · Explain · Link. A paragraph structure that forces a claim to be supported and then connected to something that matters.

Why it helps

Design command terms are justify, analyse and critically evaluate — all of which mean "make a claim and prove it". Most students lose marks not because they lack evidence, but because the evidence sits in a different paragraph from the claim. PEEL welds them together. A i A iv B iii C iv D ii

How to apply

  1. Point — one sentence stating the judgement. "The refill point location is the main barrier."
  2. Evidence — the number, quote or test result, with its source. "My three-day tally showed 14 of 30 students gave up."
  3. Explain — why that evidence supports the point, not just a restatement. "Because the queue never formed, distance rather than demand is the constraint."
  4. Link — back to the user, the specification point, or the next decision.

Design-specific rule

In Criteria B, C and D the L must link to a numbered specification point. That single rule turns loose reflection into assessable evaluation.

OPVL

From IB History 8 min per source

Origin · Purpose · Value · Limitation. A source-evaluation tool that judges evidence by where it came from and why it was made.

Why it helps

Design students collect sources but rarely weigh them. OPVL gives them the vocabulary to say why a vendor whitepaper is weaker than a government dataset — which is exactly the analytical move that separates listing research from analysing it. It also transfers to Criterion D, where students must judge their own test data. A ii A iii D i D ii

How to apply

  1. Origin — who produced it, when, and what kind of document is it?
  2. Purpose — why was it made, and who was meant to read it?
  3. Value — what does the origin and purpose let this source tell me reliably?
  4. Limitation — what can it not tell me, for exactly the same reasons?

The move most students miss

Value and limitation must both come from origin and purpose. "It is old" is not a limitation until you say why the date matters to this question. And point OPVL at their own survey too — their data also has an origin, a purpose and therefore a limitation.

A

Inquiring and analysing

Establishing that the problem is real, understood and worth solving.

Year 5 strands

  1. Explain and justify the need for a solution to a problem for a specified client or target audience.
  2. Identify and prioritize the primary and secondary research needed to develop a solution.
  3. Analyse a range of existing products that inspire a solution to the problem.
  4. Develop a detailed design brief which summarizes the analysis of relevant research.

Problem hypothesis frame

10 min

Rewrites an assumed problem as a testable claim with a named user, a suspected cause and a falsifier.

Why it helps

Strand i asks students to justify a need, not assert one. A claim written so it could be proved wrong is the only kind that can be justified with evidence. A i

How to apply

  1. Write: "I believe [user] struggle to [do what] when [situation], because [cause]."
  2. Add: "I am basing this on [what I have seen]."
  3. Add the falsifier: "If I am wrong, I would see [observation]."
  4. Check for smuggled solutions — if "app", "device" or "better" appears, rewrite.

W³D organiser

1 lesson

Who · Why · Where · Data. Four topics, three points each, converging on one problem statement.

Why it helps

Gathers everything strand i needs — client, cause, context and evidence — into one page a moderator can read at a glance. The three-point limit forces decisions rather than lists. A i A iv

How to apply

  1. Name three users: primary, secondary and the decision maker.
  2. Name three causes, each one testable.
  3. Name three physical places you could go and watch.
  4. Add three data points, each carrying a source number. Fill this box last.

Assumption dump and risk grid

12 min

Lists every unverified belief inside the problem statement, then plots them on impact against evidence held.

Why it helps

Strand ii is assessed on prioritizing research, not listing it. The grid produces that priority order from the students' own reasoning rather than from guesswork. A ii

How to apply

  1. Underline every noun and verb you have not verified.
  2. Turn each into a sticky note.
  3. Plot on two axes: impact if wrong, and evidence you already hold.
  4. Circle the top two in the high-impact, low-evidence corner. Only those get researched this week.

Five Whys

15 min

Asks "why" repeatedly to travel from a visible symptom to an underlying cause.

Why it helps

Students almost always name a symptom on day one. Designing for a symptom produces a solution that treats nothing, which then fails in Criterion D. A i

How to apply

  1. Write the symptom at the top.
  2. Ask why, five times, each answer feeding the next question.
  3. Stop when the next why leaves the student's control.
  4. Any why you cannot evidence becomes a research question for strand ii.

Fishbone (Ishikawa)

20 min

Six categories — People, Process, Place, Tools, Time, Rules — each holding three possible causes.

Why it helps

Five Whys goes deep on one path; the fishbone goes wide first, so students do not commit to the first cause they think of. Use it before Five Whys, not instead. A i

How to apply

  1. Write the problem in the fish head.
  2. Fill three causes per bone — the quota forces breadth.
  3. Mark which bones you have evidence for and which are guesses.
  4. Pick one or two likely bones and run Five Whys down them.

Problem tree

15 min

Roots are causes, the trunk is the core problem, branches are effects.

Why it helps

Shows students visually whether they picked a branch or the trunk. Designing for a branch treats a symptom; designing for a root removes the problem. A i

How to apply

  1. Write the current problem statement on the trunk.
  2. Add effects above — what goes wrong because of it.
  3. Add causes below — what makes it happen.
  4. Ask: is my trunk actually somebody else's branch? If so, move down.

Scale ladder

1 lesson

Six rungs from personal anecdote up to global data, checking where the evidence still holds.

Why it helps

Turns "is this a real problem?" into a researchable question with a defined search order, and produces the secondary research strand ii demands. A ii

How to apply

  1. Rungs: me → school → city → country → comparable countries → global.
  2. Search each rung in order, recording what you find or defending the blank.
  3. Name the highest rung with genuine evidence.
  4. Decide the verdict: same cause globally, different cause, or local only.

SCALE checklist

Ongoing

Say it in the field's words · Climb the ladder · Assess each source · Link it to us · End with a verdict.

Why it helps

The whole secondary-research process as a tick-list with an exit gate, so students can self-check before submitting rather than discovering gaps at moderation. A ii A iv

How to apply

  1. Translate everyday phrasing into the formal term researchers use.
  2. Work the ladder rung by rung, including searching for existing solutions.
  3. Complete a source card for every find — no card, no citation.
  4. Answer for each: same problem, same cause, same audience?
  5. State a verdict and say what it changes about the design.

Source card and the rule of three

5 min per source

Six fields per source, and no claim admitted unless three independent kinds of evidence support it.

Why it helps

One source is an anecdote; two of the same kind repeat one blind spot. Three different kinds cannot all be wrong in the same direction — which is what "justify" means in the descriptor. Pairs directly with OPVL. A ii A iv

How to apply

  1. Card fields: where from, when, who it studied, what it says, same as us, why I trust it.
  2. Collect one number, one voice and one observation for every claim.
  3. Reject anything over five years old unless you can defend it.
  4. Run OPVL on the two most important sources.

Resource

Branksome Hall Asia — source evaluation for MYP

Pair the card with OPVL. The card records what a source says; OPVL judges how far to trust it.

ACCESS FM

From D&T20 min

Aesthetics, Cost, Customer, Environment, Size, Safety, Function, Materials — a fixed lens for taking a product apart.

Why it helps

Strand iii asks students to analyse existing products, but without a lens they describe instead. Eight headings force analysis in eight directions and carry straight into the Criterion B specification. A iii B i

How to apply

  1. Pick three existing products that solve a similar problem.
  2. Write one analytical sentence per heading — not a description, a judgement.
  3. End each product with: what should I steal, and what should I avoid?
  4. Carry the answers forward as specification points.

Resource

What is ACCESS FM — explainer

ACCESS FM is a long-standing D&T teaching convention rather than a framework with one owning body, so no single authoritative source exists. Letters vary slightly between schools — agree your version with the department.

DVF lens

5 min

Desirable, feasible, viable — a fast triage before committing a week of research.

Why it helps

Catches problems that are really preferences, and ideas that cannot be built in the time available, before they consume the unit. A i

How to apply

  1. Desirable — does anyone other than me feel this?
  2. Feasible — could a solution be made with our tools and time?
  3. Viable — is it worth solving, and why has nobody fixed it already?
  4. Any fail sends the problem back to the hypothesis stage.

Resource

UXPin — assumptions mapping and the DVF lens

Minimum viable test

Under 1 hour

The simplest, fastest evidence-gathering that could change your mind about the riskiest assumption.

Why it helps

Produces genuine primary research inside a school timetable, and keeps strand ii research plans realistic rather than aspirational. A ii

How to apply

  1. Choose two options: tally observation, three six-minute interviews, a five-question form, shadowing one user, existing records, or one secondary statistic.
  2. Ask "tell me about the last time", never "would you use".
  3. Finish before the next lesson — if you cannot, it is the wrong test.
  4. Bring raw data, not conclusions.

B

Developing ideas

Turning research into a specification, a spread of ideas, and one justified choice.

Year 5 strands

  1. Develop design specifications which clearly state the success criteria for the design of a solution.
  2. Develop a range of feasible design ideas which can be correctly interpreted by others.
  3. Present the final chosen design and justify its selection.
  4. Develop accurate and detailed planning drawings or diagrams and outline the requirements for creation.

Testable specification writing

1 lesson

Every specification point written so it can be measured, with the test named alongside it.

Why it helps

Strand i is the hinge of the whole cycle: Criterion D evaluates against these points. A specification that cannot be tested makes a good Criterion D impossible. B i D ii

How to apply

  1. Use "shall", not "should" — it forces commitment and specificity.
  2. Attach a number or an observable outcome to every point.
  3. Write the test method in the next column at the same time.
  4. Trace each point back to a research finding from Criterion A.

MoSCoW prioritisation

10 min

Must have, Should have, Could have, Won't have this time.

Why it helps

Stops the flat specification list where twenty points all appear equally important. Priority makes the Criterion D evaluation meaningful, because failing a "could" is not the same as failing a "must". B i D ii

How to apply

  1. Sort every specification point into the four buckets.
  2. Cap the "musts" — if everything is a must, nothing is.
  3. Record the "won't" list too; it shows deliberate scoping.
  4. Evaluate musts first in Criterion D.

Agile Business Consortium — what is MoSCoW prioritisation Agile Business Consortium — MoSCoW in the DSDM handbook

Crazy 8s

8 min

Eight sketched ideas in eight minutes, one per folded panel.

Why it helps

Strand ii asks for a range of feasible ideas. Time pressure defeats the student who draws one polished idea and calls it done. B ii

How to apply

  1. Fold A3 into eight panels; one minute per panel, no erasing.
  2. Sketch only — words are labels, not substitutes.
  3. Annotate afterwards so others can interpret them, as the strand requires.
  4. Circle the three most different, not the three best.

SCAMPER

20 min

Substitute, Combine, Adapt, Modify, Put to other use, Eliminate, Reverse.

Why it helps

Gives students a way to generate variety on demand rather than waiting for inspiration, and it works directly on the products analysed in strand A iii. B ii

How to apply

  1. Start from an existing product you analysed with ACCESS FM.
  2. Apply each of the seven prompts in turn, one idea minimum each.
  3. Keep the absurd ones — they often unlock a workable variant.
  4. Filter for feasibility afterwards, never during.

Weighted decision matrix

20 min

Score each idea against weighted specification points to produce a defensible ranking.

Why it helps

Strand iii requires students to justify the selection. "I liked it best" is not justification; a matrix tied to the specification is. B iii

How to apply

  1. Rows are ideas, columns are the "must" specification points.
  2. Weight each column 1–3 by importance to the user.
  3. Score, multiply, total — then write a PEEL paragraph explaining the winner.
  4. If the matrix picks an idea you dislike, say so and explain why. That is analysis.

Planning drawing conventions

1–2 lessons

Dimensioned orthographic views, exploded diagrams, or wireframes and storyboards for digital solutions.

Why it helps

Strand iv is assessed on accuracy and sufficiency for someone else to build from. The test is literal: hand it to a peer and see whether they could make it. B iv

How to apply

  1. Physical products: dimensioned views plus an exploded assembly.
  2. Digital products: navigation map, wireframes, or a storyboard.
  3. Add a requirements list — materials, tools, components, quantities.
  4. Peer test: can they list what to buy and the first three steps?

C

Creating the solution

Planning the making, doing it well, and accounting honestly for what changed.

Year 5 strands

  1. Construct a logical plan which describes the efficient use of time and resources, sufficient for peers to follow.
  2. Demonstrate excellent technical skills when making the solution.
  3. Follow the plan to create the solution, which functions as intended.
  4. Fully justify changes made to the chosen design and plan when making the solution.

Production plan table

1 lesson

Step, time estimate, tools, materials, safety note and quality check — one row per operation.

Why it helps

Strand i is judged on whether a peer could follow it. A table makes omissions visible in a way prose hides. C i

How to apply

  1. One row per operation, in order, verbs first.
  2. Estimate time per row and total it against lessons available.
  3. Add a quality check column: how will I know this step worked?
  4. Swap plans with a partner and have them find the missing step.

Resource

VistaThink — MYP Design Criterion C

Gantt chart

20 min

Tasks against a timeline, showing what runs in parallel and what blocks what.

Why it helps

Strand i specifies efficient use of time. A list cannot show efficiency; only a timeline can show overlap, slack and dependency. C i

How to apply

  1. Columns are lessons, not dates — that is the real unit of school time.
  2. Mark dependencies: what cannot start until something else finishes.
  3. Identify anything with a lead time, such as ordering or drying.
  4. Update it in a second colour as reality diverges. The divergence is evidence for strand iv.

Process log with timestamps

5 min per lesson

Dated photographs plus a one-line note of what was done and what went wrong.

Why it helps

Strands ii and iii are evidenced almost entirely through documentation. Students who photograph only the finished article have no evidence of skill or process. C ii C iii

How to apply

  1. Photograph at the start and end of every session, always including your hands or the tool in use.
  2. Caption each with the date and the technique being demonstrated.
  3. Photograph the failures too — they are the evidence for strand iv.
  4. Keep it in one place, in order. Sorting fifty photos in week eight is a lost lesson.

Resource

VistaThink — MYP Design Criterion C

No external framework needed. The requirement comes straight from the strand: technical skill has to be visible to be assessed.

Modification log

Ongoing

A running table: what changed, why, what the alternative was, and what it cost.

Why it helps

Strand iv asks students to fully justify each change. Reconstructing reasons from memory in the last week produces vague answers; logging at the moment of change produces specific ones. C iv

How to apply

  1. Columns: date, what changed, why, what I rejected, effect on the specification.
  2. Write the row the day the change happens.
  3. Use PEEL for the "why" — evidence, not preference.
  4. Flag any change that weakens a "must" specification point.

Plan-Do-Check-Act

Per iteration

A short loop of planning a step, doing it, checking against the specification, then adjusting.

Why it helps

Keeps testing inside the making rather than saving it all for Criterion D, so failures are caught while there is still time to fix them. C iii C iv

How to apply

  1. Plan the next small stage and name the specification point it serves.
  2. Do it.
  3. Check against that point immediately, with a photo.
  4. Act — adjust, and record the adjustment in the modification log.

D

Evaluating

Testing properly, judging honestly, and saying what it meant for the user.

Year 5 strands

  1. Design detailed and relevant testing methods which generate data to measure the success of the solution.
  2. Critically evaluate the success of the solution against the design specification.
  3. Explain how the solution could be improved.
  4. Explain the impact of the solution on the client or target audience.

Testing methods matrix

1 lesson

Each specification point paired with the test that measures it and the data it generates.

Why it helps

Strand i requires methods that generate data. A matrix built from the specification guarantees relevance, which is the word doing the work in the descriptor. D i

How to apply

  1. Columns: specification point, test method, data type, who tests, what result means success.
  2. Every "must" point needs a test. No orphans in either direction.
  3. Mix quantitative and qualitative — a number and a voice.
  4. Write the success threshold before testing, not after.

Usability testing with think-aloud

15 min per user

A real user attempts a real task while narrating their thinking; you watch and stay silent.

Why it helps

Generates the qualitative data strand i asks for, and surfaces improvement evidence for strand iii that no questionnaire would find. D i D iii

How to apply

  1. Give a task, not instructions: "log a fault you had this week."
  2. Ask them to say what they are thinking. Then do not help.
  3. Record time taken, errors, hesitations and exact words.
  4. Three users will expose most major problems.

Point-by-point specification audit

1 lesson

Every specification point judged met, partly met or not met, with the evidence beside it.

Why it helps

Strand ii says against the design specification — so the specification must structure the evaluation. Students who write flowing prose about how it went usually miss half the points. D ii

How to apply

  1. One row per specification point, in the original order.
  2. Verdict, then the test data that proves the verdict.
  3. Write a PEEL paragraph for every "partly" and every "not met".
  4. Being critical about your own work scores; defending it does not.

OPVL on your own data

From IB History10 min

The same four questions turned on the student's own test results.

Why it helps

"Critically evaluate" includes evaluating the evidence itself. A student who can say why their own sample of five friends limits the conclusion is demonstrating exactly the criticality strand ii rewards. D i D ii

How to apply

  1. Origin — who did I test, when, and how were they chosen?
  2. Purpose — what was I hoping to find out, and did that shape my questions?
  3. Value — what can this data legitimately tell me?
  4. Limitation — what can it not tell me, and what would fix that next time?

Before-and-after against the baseline

30 min

The primary data collected in Criterion A becomes the benchmark the solution is measured against.

Why it helps

Strand iv asks for impact on the client. Impact is a change, and a change needs a starting number. This closes the loop on the whole unit. D iv

How to apply

  1. Retrieve the original tally, timing or count from Criterion A.
  2. Repeat that exact measurement with the solution in use.
  3. Report both numbers together, and be honest if nothing moved.
  4. Add one user quote describing the difference in their own words.

What · So what · Now what

15 min

A three-step reflection that pushes description into consequence and then into action.

Why it helps

Strand iii wants improvements explained, not listed. This structure forces students past "I would make it neater" into a reasoned change with a cause. D iii

How to apply

  1. What — what happened in testing, factually.
  2. So what — why it matters to the user and which specification point it touches.
  3. Now what — the specific change, and how you would test that it worked.
  4. Rank improvements by user impact, not by ease.

Three things worth saying to the class

Frameworks are scaffolds, not deliverables. A fishbone in the eportfolio scores nothing by itself. What scores is the sentence it let the student write. Ask for the finding, not the diagram.

PEEL and OPVL come from other subjects, and that is the point. Naming their origin out loud — English, History — makes the transfer visible and gives students a reason to trust tools they already half-know. It also lets your Humanities and English colleagues reinforce the same vocabulary.

The specification is the spine. Criterion B strand i is where Criterion D is won or lost. A specification point that cannot be tested is a mark that cannot be earned, which is why the testing method belongs in the specification table from the very first draft.
