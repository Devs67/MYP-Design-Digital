Appendix, Citations & Bibliography

How to build your appendix, cite a source properly, and format your Bibliography — the mechanics that sit underneath every criterion, checked against the current MYP Design Guide.

Simple — always shown Go deeper — tap to expand

0 · Why this matters

This is what makes the work yours

When you hand in your Design ePortfolio, you are telling your teacher: “I made this. These are my ideas.” A citation is how you prove that is true when part of what you used — a survey answer, a sensor spec, a sentence from a website — came from somewhere else. It is not extra homework. It is the difference between “I found this and used it honestly” and “I copied this and hoped no one would notice.”

Go deeper the IB regulation behind this

“All work submitted for school-based assessment — including MYP ePortfolios — must be the candidate's own authentic and individual work.”

“Plagiarism and collusion are unprincipled breaches of IB regulations, potentially subjecting candidates to consequences for academic misconduct.”

IB MYP Design Guide, p.48

This is not a style preference — it is an IB regulation with real consequences attached. A well-organised appendix and a correctly built Bibliography are how you make your “this is my own work” claim checkable at a glance, by a teacher or a moderator, in seconds rather than minutes.

1 · The appendix

What is the appendix?

Your Design report has a page limit. But sometimes you need to show extra evidence — a full set of survey answers, a long interview, extra sketches — that would use up too many pages if it went in the main report. The appendix is a separate space for that extra evidence. It does not count toward your main report's page limit.

Go deeper the exact limits, and what "unassessed" means

“Submission limits (examiners will not read beyond these limits): Written work — 40 A4 pages. Appendix (unassessed) — 10 A4 pages.”

IB MYP Design Guide, p.50

The critical detail students miss: examiners will not read past these limits at all. It is not that overflow pages get a lower mark — they are never seen. If your argument depends on something on page 42, it does not exist as far as grading is concerned.

The appendix itself is unassessed directly — it does not earn marks on its own — but a moderator can open it to check that a claim you made in your main report is actually backed up. It can be uploaded as a separate document from your main report.

2 · Stays or goes?

What goes in the appendix vs. what stays in your report

Ask yourself one question about anything you are deciding where to put: “If I deleted this from the appendix, would my report still make sense?”

Yes, it still makes sense

It belongs in the appendix — it is supporting evidence, not the argument itself.

No, the reader is lost without it

It belongs in the main report.

Go deeper the pattern, laid out side by side
Stays in your 40-page report (assessed)Goes in your 10-page appendix (unassessed)
Your analysis, argument, and justified decisionsRaw survey data / full survey results
The conclusions you drew from researchFull interview transcripts
Your final design specification reasoningExtra sketches or iterations not central to your argument
Evaluation against your own success criteriaExtended calculations, datasheets, raw test data

The pattern: the report holds your thinking. The appendix holds the raw material that thinking is built on.

3 · Numbering

Choosing your numbering system

Every item in the appendix needs a label, like a name tag, so someone can find it fast. Pick one system for your whole appendix and never switch partway through — mixing systems is confusing and looks careless.

SequentialNumber items in the order you first mention them. Appendix 1, 2, 3, 4…
By criterionGroup items under the criterion they support. Appendix A1, A2, B1, B2, C1…
Go deeper three additions that make either system work in practice

Both systems are valid — this is a strategic choice, not an IB requirement either way.

  • Appendix index. Start your appendix with a one-page contents list: label, short title, page number — a table of contents just for your evidence.
  • Page-numbering consistency. Decide once whether your appendix pages continue your main report's page numbers, or start their own sequence with a prefix — then stick with it.
  • Mirror it digitally. If you are building an ePortfolio, the order of pages/files on the platform should match your appendix index exactly. A reader should not have to guess why item A3 is filed after item B1.

4 · The golden rule

Label, cross-reference, caption

Every time you put something in the appendix, do three things.

Step 1

Give it a label

A code that matches your numbering system.

Step 2

Cross-reference it

In your main report, point to it by name: “(see Appendix A3)”.

Step 3

Caption it

In the appendix itself, write one sentence explaining what it is and why it matters.

Go deeper cross-referencing methods, a worked example, and the #1 trap

Two accepted cross-referencing methods

Inline parenthetical“(Refer Appendix A1)” placed right after the claim it supports.
Superscript footnoteA small marker in the text, linked to a footnote key.

Pick one and stay consistent. In a digital ePortfolio, your cross-reference can be a hyperlink — one click takes the reader straight to the evidence, instead of them flipping pages.

Worked example

Main body: “The target group identified portability as the priority concern (see Appendix A3).”

Appendix: “Appendix A3 — Full user survey responses (n = 24), referenced on page 12 to support the identified need.”

The #1 trap: a page pointer is not a citation

✗ Wrong

“…as shown in my research (Refer Appendix C).” This tells the reader where to look, but not what they are looking at or who made it.

✓ Right

“…as shown in my research from a nutritionist interview (Refer Appendix A1).”

The rule to repeat until it sticks: every reference needs a source named, every single time — never just a page pointer on its own.

5 · Citation content

What a citation needs, and the no-copy-paste rule

Whenever you use information from somewhere else — a website, an interview, a datasheet — you need to be able to answer: What is it called? Who made it? When was it made or published? Who published it? (And for books specifically: what is its ISBN?)

You also cannot just copy sentences from a website into your report. Say the idea in your own words, and explain why it matters to your design.

Go deeper the five-element checklist and the IB quote on paraphrasing

The five things a complete reference needs, every time: Title, Author, Publication date, Publisher, and — for books only — ISBN. Simple test: could a reader find this exact source using only what you wrote? If not, it is incomplete.

Sourcing note This five-element list comes from the IB's older “From principles into practice” document rather than the current Design Guide — treat it as strong, historically-grounded guidance rather than a quoted current rule.

“Students should concisely summarize only the useful and relevant information they have found through their research. They will present this information in their own words. Students should not copy and paste information from sources without analysis or indicating relevance.”

IB MYP Design Guide, Notes for Criterion A, p.46

7 · The bibliography

Bibliography basics: naming it and arranging it

A citation is the short pointer you use in the moment — just enough to say “this came from somewhere.” A Bibliography is the full list, gathered once at the end of your report, with one complete entry for every outside source you used anywhere in your portfolio.

  1. Alphabetical order, by the author's last name (no author? alphabetise by title instead).
  2. Hanging indent — the first line of each entry sits at the margin; every line after that is indented.
  3. One single list — never split it up by criterion, page, or source type.
  4. Double-spaced, same as the rest of your document.
Open question · not yet settled

Where does the Bibliography physically go?

This is a genuine, unresolved call, not an IB rule either way. Neither the current Design Guide nor the older “From principles into practice” document says where the Bibliography should be placed.

  • One view (the recommended default here): put it inside your 10-page appendix. This protects your 40-page main-report limit.
  • An alternative view, from a separate school workshop resource: the Bibliography is not part of the appendix at all — it is its own required third section, placed after it (Main Body → Appendix → Bibliography). No IB source is cited for this position either.

Confirm your department's actual policy before treating either answer as settled.

Go deeper "Bibliography" vs "Works Cited" — the naming note

Your school's heading may be “Bibliography,” formatted in MLA 9 style. Technically, MLA's own term for this page is “Works Cited” (only sources actually cited in the text) — “Bibliography” (sources consulted, even if not directly cited) is more of a Chicago-style term. Use whichever heading your teacher specifies — the important thing is consistency, not the label itself.

8 · MLA 9 mechanics

MLA 9 core elements, and the anatomy of a citation

MLA 9 (the citation style used here) has nine possible pieces of information you could include in a citation. You only use the ones that actually apply to your source, in this order, then stop:

1. Author · 2. Title of Source · 3. Title of Container · 4. Other Contributors · 5. Version · 6. Number · 7. Publisher · 8. Publication Date · 9. Location

One rule to remember: if the Author and Publisher are the same organisation, drop the Publisher — do not list it twice.

10 · Formatting

Setting the hanging indent

A “hanging indent” means the first line of each Bibliography entry starts at the margin, and every line after that is pushed in half an inch. Do not fake this by pressing Tab or Space — use your word processor's actual indent setting, so it stays correct no matter how the text reflows.

Go deeper the how-to, Google Docs and Word
Google Docs
  • Select all your citations
  • Format → Align & indent → Indentation options
  • Special indent → Hanging → 0.5″
Microsoft Word
  • Select all your citations
  • Home → Paragraph group → corner arrow
  • Indentation → Special → Hanging → By: 0.5″

11 · Tools

Citation tools — the links page

You do not have to build every citation by hand. These tools will generate one for you — but always double-check what they give you before it goes in your Bibliography. Tools get details wrong more often than you would expect.

Not hypothetical caution: while preparing this exact set of teaching materials, an AI tool produced a citation with a redundant, duplicated publisher and a wrong element list — both had to be caught and corrected by hand. If a generator can get it wrong while making the lesson about getting it right, it can get it wrong for a student too. Verify, always.
